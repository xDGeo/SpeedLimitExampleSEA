# Weather-responsive and winter variable speed limit deployments: limits set, triggers, measured effects

Provenance tags on every figure. **[P]** = I read it in the primary document's full text. **[A]** = I read only the official abstract or bibliographic record of the primary (for example VTT CRIS, TRID, ROSA P landing page), not the full text. **[S]** = I saw it only as quoted in another document, named in the bullet; the original was not read. **[X]** = I saw it only in a search-engine excerpt; the page could not be opened, so treat it as unverified. Researched 9 November 2026. mph-to-km/h conversions in brackets are mine.

## Finland: E18 weather-controlled limits and the 100 to 80 km/h winter limit

### Takeaway
The seasonal 100 to 80 km/h limit is the strongest Finnish result: about 3.8 km/h lower mean speed and a statistically significant 14% fall in all accidents in the original trial. The weather-controlled systems produced small speed effects (1.8 to 5.3 km/h beyond weather), and their injury-accident effects were never statistically significant and differed in sign by system type.

### Cited Findings

**Name check.** The brief's "Ramo 1999" is Rämä, P. (1999), "Effects of weather-controlled variable speed limits and warning signs on driver behavior", *Transportation Research Record* 1689(1), 53-59. DOI [10.3141/1689-08](https://doi.org/10.3141/1689-08).

**Seasonal limit, speed and crashes**
- Trial design: two winters and two summers, November 1987 to September 1989; 100 km/h limits on selected roads lowered to 80 km/h in winter in two phases; radar speeds of more than 200,000 vehicles and more than 3,000 driver interviews, three in four supporting. [A] — [Peltola 1991, PTRC Proceedings P350, pp. 193-198, TRID record](https://trid.trb.org/View/375952)
- Lowering to 80 km/h cut average speed of all vehicles by 3.8 km/h; weather and road conditions lowered winter average speed by a further 3 km/h; raising the summer limit to 100 km/h added 3.7 km/h. [A] — [Peltola 1991, TRID record](https://trid.trb.org/View/375952)
- All accidents fell 14% with the winter limit, with the largest effect when the road was not dry; summer increases to 100 km/h raised accidents, at least on the busiest roads in good conditions. [A] — [Peltola 1991, TRID record](https://trid.trb.org/View/375952)
- Peltola (2000) reports the 14% as statistically significant, from a mean speed change of only 3.8 km/h; says the follow-up covering winters 1989-1996 suggests a larger reduction; and says gains were greatest in good conditions on higher-standard roads where the limit fell the most. Stated justifications for lower winter limits include slippery surfaces, studded tyres, heavy rain or snow, and darkness. [A] — [Peltola 2000, TRR 1734(1), 46-51, DOI 10.3141/1734-07, VTT record](https://cris.vtt.fi/en/publications/seasonally-changing-speed-limits-effects-on-speeds-and-accidents/)
- Injury accidents specifically: -11% in the 1987-89 trial, *not* statistically significant; a later follow-up found a significant fall, "most plausibly" as much as -28% (citing Peltola 1991b and Peltola 1997). [S, in Rämä et al. 2003] — [Rämä, Schirokoff, Rajamäki 2003, Finnra Reports 54/2003](https://www.tieh.fi/julkaisut/pdf/3200841_vms-turva.pdf)
- Cars -5.2 km/h and all vehicles -3.8 km/h with the seasonal drop; injury accidents estimated -28% "during the experiment". [S, in Rämä 2001] — [Rämä 2001, VTT Publications 447](https://sarjaweb.vtt.fi/pdf/publications/2001/P447.pdf). **Conflict:** Rämä (2003) attaches 28% to the later follow-up, and -11% (non-significant) to the experiment; Rämä (2001) attaches 28% to the experiment.
- 2010-2014 re-analysis (Peltola, *Liikenneviraston tutkimuksia ja selvityksiä* 61/2015): 36 injury accidents and 8 deaths avoided each winter. [S, conference slides; the table itself is an image I could not read] — [Klang 2018, NVF slides](https://nvfnorden.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/09-Safety-effects-of-lower-speed-limits-during-winter-months-Jaakko-Klang-FIN.pdf)
- Same report as quoted in a 2026 agency press release: -15% injury accidents and -29% deaths on affected roads, "nearly 40" accidents and "about ten" deaths a year. [S] — [Pohjois-Suomen elinvoimakeskus press release, 27 Oct 2026](https://www.sttinfo.fi/tiedote/72447134/talvi-ja-pimean-ajan-nopeusrajoitukset-tulevat-voimaan-talla-viikolla?lang=fi). **Conflict:** 36 and 8 (slides) against nearly 40 and about 10 (release), both citing the same report. The report's repository page returned HTTP 403: [Doria handle](https://www.doria.fi/handle/10024/121607).
- Current practice, autumn 2026: limits take effect 28 October with signs changed nationally by 30 October; in Northern Ostrobothnia and Kainuu about 1,600 km of 100 km/h road drops to 80, about 180 km of 80 km/h road drops to 70, and about 3 km drops to 60. [P for the release] — [press release, 27 Oct 2026](https://www.sttinfo.fi/tiedote/72447134/talvi-ja-pimean-ajan-nopeusrajoitukset-tulevat-voimaan-talla-viikolla?lang=fi)
- Winter risk context: injury risk estimated at over nine times higher on snowy and 20 times higher on icy surfaces than on dry bare road in Finland (Polvinen 1985; Malmivuo and Peltola 1997, with limited exposure data); Norwegian estimate 2.5 times. On slippery roads only 14% of Finnish drivers judged the road slippery; average speed was only about 4 km/h lower than in good winter conditions. [S, in Rämä 2001] — [VTT Publications 447](https://sarjaweb.vtt.fi/pdf/publications/2001/P447.pdf)

**E18 weather-controlled limit, driver behaviour**
- Setting: E18 test site, two unmanned road weather stations, automatic limit changes, loop-detector speed and headway data. Winter change from 100 to 80 km/h cut free-flow car mean speed by 3.4 km/h, on top of a 6.3 km/h average fall from weather and road surface. [A] — [Rämä 1999, VTT record](https://cris.vtt.fi/en/publications/effects-of-weather-controlled-variable-speed-limits-and-warning-s/); 3.4 km/h confirmed in [Rämä 2001](https://sarjaweb.vtt.fi/pdf/publications/2001/P447.pdf) [P]
- When poor conditions were hard to detect (no or insignificant rain or snow) the effect was 1.9 km/h higher, 5.3 km/h in total. [A] — [Rämä 1999, VTT record](https://cris.vtt.fi/en/publications/effects-of-weather-controlled-variable-speed-limits-and-warning-s/)
- When slippery-road signs were also shown, the variable limit cut mean speed by only 1.8 km/h, against 9.3 km/h from the weather itself. Variance fell, headways showed no notable change, and speeds also dropped on the adjacent unequipped section. [A] — [Rämä 1999, VTT record](https://cris.vtt.fi/en/publications/effects-of-weather-controlled-variable-speed-limits-and-warning-s/)
- Slippery-road and minimum-headway signs alone cut free-flow car speed by 1-2 km/h. [P] — [Rämä 2001](https://sarjaweb.vtt.fi/pdf/publications/2001/P447.pdf)
- Road 7 motorway (120 km/h summer, 100 km/h winter): lowering to 80 km/h in bad weather cut overall mean speed by 2.5 km/h beyond the weather effect; the low limit ran about 20% of studied time; the speed change "would correspond to" about -8% accidents during that time (a model estimate). [S, in Rämä et al. 2003, citing Rämä 1997 and 2001] — [Finnra 54/2003](https://www.tieh.fi/julkaisut/pdf/3200841_vms-turva.pdf)
- Raising the limit to 100 km/h in good conditions raised mean speed by about 4 km/h on road 7 and about 6 km/h on road 1. [S, same] — [Finnra 54/2003](https://www.tieh.fi/julkaisut/pdf/3200841_vms-turva.pdf)

**Weather-controlled systems, injury accidents**
- Design: before-after with comparison roads of the same type on fixed limits; eight two-lane sections, 8-41 km long, follow-up summer 1990 to winter 2001-02. [P] — [Rämä, Schirokoff, Rajamäki 2003](https://www.tieh.fi/julkaisut/pdf/3200841_vms-turva.pdf)
- All eight combined: injury accident risk -4% in winter and +9% in summer. Four E18 systems (fibre-optic or LED signs, automatic road-condition classification, variable slippery warnings): -13% winter, -2% summer. Four road 4 and 9 systems (electromechanical signs, no automatic classification, no variable warnings): **+8% winter, +21% summer**. None statistically significant; the authors call the results indicative only. [P] — [Finnra 54/2003](https://www.tieh.fi/julkaisut/pdf/3200841_vms-turva.pdf); same -13% and -2% in [Rämä and Schirokoff 2004, VTT record](https://cris.vtt.fi/en/publications/effects-of-weather-controlled-variable-speed-limits-on-injury-acc/) [A]
- Two more years of data, 14 years total: about -10% injury accident risk, still not statistically significant. [P] — [Schirokoff, Rämä, Tuomainen 2006, "County-wide variable speed limits?", SIRWEC, Turin](https://sirwec.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Torino-D-22.pdf)
- Usage explains part of the split: the 100 km/h setting ran about 10% of total time on E18 but 45-68% of winter time on roads 4 and 9; the lowest 60 or 70 km/h settings ran only about 1-3% of winter time. [P] — [Finnra 54/2003](https://www.tieh.fi/julkaisut/pdf/3200841_vms-turva.pdf)
- Model used in Finnish appraisal: 1 km/h lower mean speed gives -2.5% injury accidents, 1 km/h higher gives +3.7% (Ranta and Kallberg 1996); a 20 km/h limit change typically moves mean speed 4-8 km/h. [S, in Finnra 54/2003] — [Finnra 54/2003](https://www.tieh.fi/julkaisut/pdf/3200841_vms-turva.pdf)

### Inferences
- The E18 group's -13% cannot be attributed to the limit alone: it also differs in sign technology, automation and warning signs, and all three vary together.
- The speed response is about 0.17 to 0.19 km/h per 1 km/h of posted reduction (3.4 to 3.8 km/h for a 20 km/h change). Peltola's 14% is larger than the 9.5% the Ranta and Kallberg slope would give for 3.8 km/h, so either the seasonal limit acts on more than mean speed (for example the upper tail) or it is partly confounded.
- Both the seasonal and variable results describe a change *to* 80 km/h from 100 km/h, not a further reduction below an 80 km/h base.

### Gaps
- I could not open the full text of Rämä 1999, Peltola 2000 or Peltola 2015; figures for those come from abstracts or quoted summaries. The VTI copy of Rämä et al. (2001), "The effects of weather controlled speed limits on driver behaviour" ([VTT record](https://cris.vtt.fi/en/publications/the-effects-of-weather-controlled-speed-limits-on-driver-behaviou/)), downloaded but yielded no extractable text.
- The Rämä and Schirokoff study covers two-lane roads only. Schirokoff et al. (2006) state the results cannot be generalised to motorways.

## Sweden: Trafikverket and VTI evaluations

### Takeaway
Sweden gives one controlled-comparison winter trial with mixed crash results and a secondary report of very large speed reductions on ice at weather-controlled sites. I found no Swedish crash evaluation specific to weather-controlled variable limits, and no published thresholds.

### Cited Findings
- Seasonal trial winter 1999-2000: 90 to 70 km/h on 69 sections (652 km) and 110 to 90 km/h on 70 sections (1,113 km), 1 November to 24 April in the north and to 15 March elsewhere; comparison roads with unchanged limits. [P] — [Andersson 2000, VTI notat 58-2000](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:669972/FULLTEXT01.pdf)
- 90 to 70 km/h: mean speed of cars -4 km/h (3-5 regionally), trucks with trailers -2.3 km/h. Injury accidents 33.6 per winter before against 25 during; injured 61 against 51; comparison roads 961.4 against 996 injury accidents. [P] — [VTI notat 58-2000](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:669972/FULLTEXT01.pdf)
- 110 to 90 km/h: mean speed of cars -4.2 km/h; trucks with trailers (limit 80) about +1 km/h on average. Injury accidents **rose** from 72.8 to 88 (+20.9%, outside the before-period 95% interval 58.8-86.8), injured 119 to 167 (+40.3%), while comparison roads fell from 35.6 to 23 (-35.4%). [P] — [VTI notat 58-2000](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:669972/FULLTEXT01.pdf)
- VTI's own conclusion: accident development was "not unambiguous", and more trials were needed. Selection problem: 110 km/h trial roads had far lower pre-trial risk (0.07 against 0.13 injury accidents per million axle-pair km) and were not chosen at random. [P] — [VTI notat 58-2000](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:669972/FULLTEXT01.pdf)
- Vägverket trials of variable limits ran from 2003 at 20 sites; applications included junctions, pedestrians, weather (mainly road condition and wind) and traffic intensity. At weather-controlled sites, raising the top limit by 10 or 20 km/h in good conditions raised mean speed by only 2-4 km/h. **On ice and severe ice slipperiness mean speed fell 12-20 km/h below the speed drivers spontaneously choose.** [S, in VTI notat 6-2012, citing Vägverket 2008a] — [Gustafsson et al. 2012, VTI notat 6-2012](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:669257/FULLTEXT01.pdf)
- A weather model computing surface friction and wind notified operators when to activate manually; fewer motorists exceeded the limit by 10 km/h than before. [S, in Al-Kaisy et al. 2012, citing Peterson 2007] — [Al-Kaisy, A., Ewan, L., Veneziano, D., June 2012, "Evaluation of a Variable Speed Limit System for Wet and Extreme Weather Conditions: Phase 1 Report", SPR 743, Western Transportation Institute for Oregon DOT; cited below as SPR 743 Phase 1](https://westerntransportationinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/03/4W3603_SPR743_VSL_System.pdf)
- In 2012 four stretches had weather-controlled limits: E22 Blekinge, Ölandsbron, E6 Halland and Uddevallabron. The report identifies candidate sites from interviews and weather and crash data; it does not report evaluation results. [P] — [Trafikverket 2012:153, Behovsinventering av väderstyrd variabel hastighet](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:1363816/FULLTEXT01.pdf)
- Trafikverket says accident risk has decreased, traffic has calmed and flow improved "somewhat" where variable limits are used; no numbers given. [P] — [Trafikverket, Variabla hastigheter](https://www.trafikverket.se/resa-och-trafik/trafiksakerhet/sakerhet-pa-vag/hastighetsgranser-pa-vag/variabla-hastigheter/)
- Not weather, but relevant to a 90 km/h base: at a rural junction with detector-triggered 90 or 70 km/h, speeds fell 7.3 km/h at 90 km/h and 16.6 km/h at 70 km/h; the crash benefit was only predicted by a power model. [S, in SPR 743, citing Towliat et al. 2006] — [SPR 743 Phase 1](https://westerntransportationinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/03/4W3603_SPR743_VSL_System.pdf)

### Inferences
- The 12-20 km/h ice result is far larger than Finland's 1.8 km/h when slippery signs were displayed. The two are not directly comparable (different baselines and sites), and the Swedish one has only reached me second-hand, so it should not be used as a design value without the source.
- The 110 to 90 km/h result shows a single-winter crash count is too noisy to be read either way; it does not show that lower limits raise crashes.

### Gaps
- Vägverket 2008a and the 2008 Landefors Via Nordica presentation could not be retrieved (DNS failure). Activation thresholds, displayed limit sets and crash outcomes at the four weather sites remain unverified.

## United States: FHWA guidance, Wyoming, Utah, Washington, Oregon and other programmes

### Takeaway
US weather VSL deployments are mostly freeways with 65-75 mph bases, stepping down in 5-10 mph increments to a floor of 30-35 mph, triggered by RWIS friction, visibility, snow and operator judgement. Wyoming I-80 is the best evidence (total CMF 0.66) but has no comparison group; the one fog-focused corridor tested in the same FHWA study showed no detectable benefit.

### Cited Findings

**Wyoming I-80**
- System: Elk Mountain corridor from February 2009, then Green River and Evanston and Laramie-Cheyenne, 143 mi in total, regulatory, set from the statewide TMC. [P] — [Saha, Ahmed, Young 2015, TRR 2521, 45-53, DOI 10.3141/2521-05](https://www.uwyo.edu/ahmed/papers/2015%20trr%20safety%20effectiveness%20of%20vsl%20systems%20in%20adverse%20weather%20conditions%20on%20challenging%20roadway%20geometry.pdf)
- Limits: maximum 75 mph [121 km/h]; signs can show 75, 65, 60, 55, 50, 45, 40 and 35 mph; WYDOT protocol is to **close the road if the recommended speed would be below 35 mph** [56 km/h]. A seasonal 65 mph limit from 15 October to 15 April applied at first and was later removed. ADT about 11,000, 50-60% trucks. [P] — [Sui and Young 2013, ITE Western District paper](https://www.westernite.org/annualmeetings/13_Phoenix/Papers/Session%205D%20-%20Sui.pdf)
- FHWA interrupted time series, Elk Mountain only: 63 segments, 92.9 directional mi, 121 months (February 2004 to February 2014), no comparison sites. CMFs: total 0.66, fatal and injury 0.49, PDO 0.71, rear-end 0.35, fixed-object 0.59 (all significant at 95%); rear-end F+I 0.34 and fixed-object F+I 0.39 not significant. B/C 9.05. [P] — [Avelar et al. 2021, FHWA-HRT-21-053](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/57267/dot_57267_DS1.pdf)
- Winter crash counts across four corridors, October 2007 to April 2012: crude -41% (Cheyenne-Laramie -24%, Elk Mountain -28%, Evanston -58%, Rock Springs and Green River -55%), explicitly without weather or traffic adjustment. A fixed-effects negative binomial model then found VSL significant in reducing winter crashes. [P] — [Saha et al. 2015](https://www.uwyo.edu/ahmed/papers/2015%20trr%20safety%20effectiveness%20of%20vsl%20systems%20in%20adverse%20weather%20conditions%20on%20challenging%20roadway%20geometry.pdf). **Internal conflict:** the results text says Elk Mountain and Green River were significant, the conclusions say only the less-challenging corridors were.
- WYDOT naive evaluation: -0.67 crashes per week per 100 mi, about 50.1 crashes a year across 143 mi; total crashes 237 (2004) to 197 (2010); fatal crashes unchanged. [S, in FHWA-HRT-21-053, citing WYDOT 2012] — [Avelar et al. 2021](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/57267/dot_57267_DS1.pdf)
- Drivers cut speed by 0.47-0.75 mph per 1 mph of posted reduction ("preliminary"). [A] — [Buddemeyer, Young, Dorsey-Spitz 2010, TRR 2189, 37-44, DOI 10.3141/2189-05](https://pure.gonzaga.edu/p/en/research-outputs/ldabd7jkhpzg-50fdca57-b672-4a98-a1e4-fac2ab189679)
- Speed above posted limit (regression at Elk Mountain): with the maximum posted, drivers ran 1.3 mph below it. Reduction of 15 mph or less: 5.4 mph above posted. Reduction of 16-25 mph: 8.5 mph above. Reduction of 30 mph or more: about 13 mph above. Trucks were 5.1 mph closer to the limit than cars. Car speed standard deviation reached about 15 mph during weather incidents. [P] — [Sui and Young 2013](https://www.westernite.org/annualmeetings/13_Phoenix/Papers/Session%205D%20-%20Sui.pdf)
- Phase II report: statistically significant crash reduction after VSL, reduced speed variation, and a "reduction in speed compliance related to large reductions in posted speed limits". [A, no figures in the abstract] — [Young, Sabawat, Saha, Sui 2012, FHWA-WY-13/03F](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/29333)
- Current WYDOT speed-setting does not take geometry into account. [P] — [Saha et al. 2015](https://www.uwyo.edu/ahmed/papers/2015%20trr%20safety%20effectiveness%20of%20vsl%20systems%20in%20adverse%20weather%20conditions%20on%20challenging%20roadway%20geometry.pdf)

**Virginia I-77, fog-focused: no detectable effect**
- 22 segments, 24.4 mi, January 2010 to December 2017, VSL from October 2016, 10-month after period, lower limits displayed about 5% of the time. No crash type changed significantly. [P] — [Avelar et al. 2021](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/57267/dot_57267_DS1.pdf)
- Table 6 reads total 1.23, **night 0.87**, F+I 1.20, PDO 1.22, rear-end 1.78, fixed-object 1.43, nondry 1.10, low-visibility 1.04. [P] **Table conflict:** the report's Table 9 summary shifts these columns (it prints 0.87 under F+I). The text confirms that night was the only reduction and that F+I rose, so use Table 6.

**Utah I-80 Parleys Canyon**
- 15 signs, 35-65 mph range, regulatory. [X] — [UDOT 2014](https://connect.udot.utah.gov/2014/01/16/variable-speed-limit-signs-now-activated-on-i-80/); about 13 mi, automated, with TMC operators and a weather group. [P] — [FHWA TMC Pooled Fund deck, Aug 2024](https://tmcpfs.ops.fhwa.dot.gov/pdfs/TMS_Managing_Use_VSLs_Adverse_Weather.pdf)
- Trigger rules: low visibility event requires visibility below 0.20 and relative humidity above 75; wind event requires gust above 45; winter-driving event requires grip decreasing from 0.82 with snow detected; standing-water event requires increasing depth above 0.25; snow event combines soil temperature and snowfall rate. Units are not stated in the text (the figures are not extractable). [P] — [Petzke, Wang, Fay 2025, Aurora Project 2022-10](https://www.intrans.iastate.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/automating_VSLs_w_cvr.pdf)
- Compliance is defined as no more than 10 mph over the displayed limit. Amber against white legends: winter compliance +9% on average, more than +20% at one sensor, no significant change in October; incident numbers about 50% lower. [P] — [Azin and Yang 2022, MPC 22-488](https://www.ugpti.org/resources/reports/downloads/mpc22-488.pdf). **Weak design:** this compares sign colours, not VSL against no VSL, and is uncontrolled. FHWA's deck repeats the 50% without that qualification.
- Earlier I-215 fog advisory system (1995-2000): speed variability -22% in inclement conditions, up to -35% in moderate fog. [S, in SPR 743, citing Perrin et al. 2002] — [SPR 743 Phase 1](https://westerntransportationinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/03/4W3603_SPR743_VSL_System.pdf)

**Washington I-90 Snoqualmie Pass and US 2**
- WSDOT operational look-up table (regulatory, tied to chain rules): **70/65 mph** dry or wet, visibility above 0.5 mi. **55 mph** [89 km/h] light snow, slush or ice in places, fog below 0.2 mi, traction tyres advised. **45 mph** [72 km/h] snow and ice, deep slush, blowing snow below 0.1 mi, traction tyres required. **35 mph** [56 km/h] severe freezing rain, deep snow, chains required. [P for the deck; it cites an unpublished 2024 WSDOT document] — [FHWA deck, Aug 2024](https://tmcpfs.ops.fhwa.dot.gov/pdfs/TMS_Managing_Use_VSLs_Adverse_Weather.pdf); same table with 65 mph top in [Petzke et al. 2025](https://www.intrans.iastate.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/automating_VSLs_w_cvr.pdf) [P]
- Original 1997 system: 65 to 35 mph in 10 mph steps. [S, in SPR 743, citing Ulfarsson et al. 2001] — [SPR 743 Phase 1](https://westerntransportationinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/03/4W3603_SPR743_VSL_System.pdf)
- Simulator: drivers given reduced limits slowed, but informed drivers tended to have higher speed deviation, which "can possibly increase the probability of accidents". Authors note that signs active only in bad weather confound any sign effect with weather. [P] — [Ulfarsson et al. 2001, TRAC T9903 Task 16](https://depts.washington.edu/trac/bulkdisk/pdf/511.1.pdf)
- Field: mean speed fell beyond the weather effect, while speed deviation fell eastbound and **rose westbound**. [X] — [Ulfarsson et al. 2005, *Int. J. Vehicle Information and Communication Systems*](https://www.inderscience.com/info/inarticle.php?artid=7586)

**Ohio I-90 Lake County (figures conflict)**
- Limits: 70 mph maximum, 30 mph minimum, 10 mph steps; examples 60 mph for moderate precipitation, 50 mph for drifting snow or low visibility; previously a seasonal 60 mph advisory. [P] — [NOCoE case study, May 2020](https://transportationops.org/case-studies/lake-90-variable-speed-limit-corridor)
- Two seasons: total crashes -22%, snow-event crashes -42% "from peak", clearance time -31 min; no control group or statistical test reported. [P] — [NOCoE 2020](https://transportationops.org/case-studies/lake-90-variable-speed-limit-corridor)
- Seven years: -35% overall; 76 to 49 crashes per year; winter crashes 37 to 21 (-43%, my arithmetic); fatal and injury 20 to 9 per year. [P for the article; ODOT report not read] — [AASHTO Journal, 2024](https://aashtojournal.transportation.org/?p=32081)
- **Conflict:** the FHWA deck says -35% for snow events on slide 6 and -58% for snow-event crashes in its notes (with -63% secondary crashes). [P] — [FHWA deck, Aug 2024](https://tmcpfs.ops.fhwa.dot.gov/pdfs/TMS_Managing_Use_VSLs_Adverse_Weather.pdf). All of these are uncontrolled before-after figures.

**Other programmes and guidance**
- Oregon (US 97, I-5, I-84): RWIS and CCTV, mostly automated, enforceable, minimum 30 mph. [P] — [FHWA deck, Aug 2024](https://tmcpfs.ops.fhwa.dot.gov/pdfs/TMS_Managing_Use_VSLs_Adverse_Weather.pdf). OR 217 is advisory, automated, combines friction, visibility and congestion. [P] — [FHWA-HOP-17-003, ch. 3](https://ops.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/fhwahop17003/ch3.htm). The ODOT SPR 743 Phase 1 report (June 2012) is a literature review and concept design, not an evaluation of a deployment. [P]
- New Jersey Turnpike: 35 mph when an operator can see three mile markers. [P] — [FHWA-HOP-17-003, ch. 3](https://ops.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/fhwahop17003/ch3.htm). 5 mph steps down to 30 mph for ice, snow and fog. [S, in SPR 743, citing Robinson 2000]
- Pennsylvania I-80 and I-81 pilots: portable signs, manual process following procedures; central office approves limits below a threshold; preliminary results "indicating that drivers are slowing down", no figures. [P] — [FHWA deck](https://tmcpfs.ops.fhwa.dot.gov/pdfs/TMS_Managing_Use_VSLs_Adverse_Weather.pdf)
- Tennessee I-75 fog: only one fog-related crash reported in the first 9 years. [S, in SPR 743, citing Goodwin 2003] — anecdotal and uncontrolled.
- Colorado derives limits from physics rather than a look-up table: braking distance held constant at 188 ft (65 mph) with friction adjusted for grade. Example outputs: friction 0.75 gives 65 mph, 0.5 gives 55, 0.35-0.4 gives 45, 0.25-0.3 gives 40, 0.2 gives 35, 0.15 gives 30, 0.1 gives 25, 0.05 gives 15, with a CDOT-defined minimum overriding the lowest values. Visibility lowers the limit in 5 mph steps until stopping distance fits. CDOT states "30 mph (or slower)" for the most slippery ice. [P] — [CDOT Variable Speed Limits Guidelines, Oct 2023](https://www.codot.gov/safety/traffic-safety/assets/variable-speed-limits/cdot-variable-speed-limits-guidelines_2023-11-02.pdf)
- FHWA view: compliance is "typically low" if limits are not enforceable; VSLs work best with message signs that give a credible reason; benefits vary by site. [P] — [FHWA deck, Aug 2024](https://tmcpfs.ops.fhwa.dot.gov/pdfs/TMS_Managing_Use_VSLs_Adverse_Weather.pdf)
- Just over half of agencies surveyed had evaluated their VSL or VSA programme, using crash data, compliance data, crash severity, personal experience and speed sensors. [P] — [Petzke et al. 2025](https://www.intrans.iastate.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/automating_VSLs_w_cvr.pdf)
- FHWA's 2017 synthesis gives system descriptions as of January 2016 and few evaluation numbers. Nevada US 395 Alternate had blank signs and inconsistent limits, and deactivation was being considered. [P] — [FHWA-HOP-17-003, ch. 3](https://ops.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/fhwahop17003/ch3.htm)

### Inferences
- Even the best US crash figures are from systems combined with other changes: enforceability, beacons, closures, public information. The Wyoming CMF is an average across all time, so it includes corridor-wide changes unrelated to limit display.
- Wyoming shows a dose-response limit: the bigger the posted drop, the larger the absolute slowing but the worse the compliance. A 30 mph drop leaves drivers 13 mph over.
- The Virginia null result reflects low power (10-month after period, active 5% of time) as much as low effect. It cannot be read as evidence that VSL does nothing in fog.

### Gaps
- I did not find a Clear Roads VSL synthesis. NCHRP Project 03-142 (real-time warnings and VSL during weather events) was described as "publication pending" in September 2025 ([Petzke et al. 2025](https://www.intrans.iastate.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/automating_VSLs_w_cvr.pdf)); I could not confirm it has been published.
- FHWA-SA-12-022 chapter 6 (weather VSL case studies) now redirects to a generic page. Goodwin 2003 could not be retrieved. The FHWA deck credits Goodwin with "Wisconsin DOT: 13 percent" while a rural ITS toolkit excerpt [X] attributes a 13% fall to WSDOT's Travel Aid; I could not resolve which is right.
- The full Wyoming Phase II report and the 2010 Elk Mountain report exceeded the fetch limit and were blocked by direct download, so WYDOT's trigger values are unverified.

## Germany, Netherlands and Austria: thresholds for reduced limits

### Takeaway
The Dutch A16 fog system is the only one of these with a fully documented threshold table and a behavioural evaluation. Germany publishes sensor classes (FGSV 2010) but I could not verify the mapping to specific limits. For Austria I found no thresholds or weather-specific evaluation.

### Cited Findings

**Netherlands A16 (near Breda)**
- Thresholds from 29 January 1992: visibility 0-70 m shows **60 km/h**; 70-140 m shows **80 km/h**; above 140 m blank. Before that date thresholds were 120 and 80 m, with a night correction. A 60 km/h sign is always preceded upstream by 80. [P] — [Hogema, van der Horst, Bakker 1994, TNO-TM 1994 C-48](https://open.rijkswaterstaat.nl/publish/pages/153378/70142.pdf)
- Evaluation February 1992 to March 1994 (790 days, loops at six locations): mean speed 8-10 km/h lower than from visibility alone; no effect around 200 m. Speed SD down slightly; little or no effect on headway and TTC. [P] — [TNO-TM 1994 C-48](https://open.rijkswaterstaat.nl/publish/pages/153378/70142.pdf)
- **Adverse effect:** below 35 m visibility, mean speed with the system was significantly *higher* than without it, because the 60 km/h display exceeded what drivers would choose on visibility alone. [P] — [TNO-TM 1994 C-48](https://open.rijkswaterstaat.nl/publish/pages/153378/70142.pdf). A figure of 60 km/h with against 29 km/h without appears only in an excerpt of a USDOT summary. [X]
- The authors estimate -15% accidents from a 5 km/h reduction using Koornstra (1990). That is a model estimate, not measured. [P] — [TNO-TM 1994 C-48](https://open.rijkswaterstaat.nl/publish/pages/153378/70142.pdf)
- A rain algorithm was proposed: 0.6-2.0 mm of water with rain intensity 2.5-6.0 mm/h gives one step down and 6-30 mm/h a second step, from a stated 75 mph base. It had not been deployed as of 2008. The report's mph values look like conversions from 120, 100 and 80 km/h, so check the original. [S, in SPR 743, citing Jonkers et al. 2008] — [SPR 743 Phase 1](https://westerntransportationinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/03/4W3603_SPR743_VSL_System.pdf)

**Germany (Streckenbeeinflussungsanlagen)**
- Visibility classes (on / off): SW1 ≤400 / >500 m; SW2 ≤250 / >300 m; SW3 ≤120 / >150 m; SW4 ≤80 / >100 m; SW5 ≤50 / >60 m. Precipitation classes (on / off): NI1 ≥0.3 / <0.2; NI2 ≥1.2 / <1.0; NI3 ≥5.0 / <4.0; NI4 ≥12.0 / <10.0 mm/h. Existing systems may use other locally adapted values. [S, tables reproduced in TUM report from FGSV 2010] — [Haug and Busch 2016, Eching Ost test field, 11th phase](https://api.lambrecht.net/upload/productDocuments/Umfelddatentestfeld_Abschlussbericht_3.pdf)
- Precipitation intensity and visibility feed automatic logic directly through thresholds. Road slipperiness is only a *switching proposal*, and slipperiness warnings are manual switchings. The brightness sensor controls sign brightness only. [P] — [Haug and Busch 2016](https://api.lambrecht.net/upload/productDocuments/Umfelddatentestfeld_Abschlussbericht_3.pdf). **Trap:** that report's "Sichtweite SW 60" is data type code 60, not a 60 m threshold; a search summary I saw misread it.
- Baden-Württemberg: limits are set from surface temperature, precipitation, visibility, dew point and water film; the range runs from one 120 km/h sign at wetness level 1 to two 60 km/h signs with slippery warning at level 5. [X, page renders only with JavaScript] — [SVZ-BW](https://www.svz-bw.de/verkehrsbeeinflussungsanlagen/streckenbeeinflussung/technik-und-wirkung)
- Accident trends were positive before-after for all systems studied, including against the whole motorway network, but data allowed effects of specific conditions to be described only qualitatively. Rain detection had problems with intensity measurement and moisture classes. Recommends 5-12 visibility classes, 5 precipitation classes and 2 lighting classes. [A] — [Mangold, Traeger, Lindenbach 1996, FGSV Heft 729, TRID record](https://trid.trb.org/View/988788)
- Displays of 100, 80 or 60 km/h for traffic and environmental data; crash rates -20 to -30% after deployment. [S, in SPR 743, citing Robinson 2000; mixed traffic and weather, no design given] — [SPR 743 Phase 1](https://westerntransportationinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/03/4W3603_SPR743_VSL_System.pdf)
- On German motorways, precipitation raised accident rate on average by 30%, while accident cost rate fell 22%. Snow and ice could not be analysed for lack of data (travel-time section of the report). [P] — [Friedrich, Pillat, Lohmiller 2014, WOLKE](https://edocs.tib.eu/files/e01fb14/802928994.pdf)

**Austria (ASFINAG VBA)**
- Salzburg system, 52 km, full operation from March 2015: sensors and weather stations (precipitation, wind, visibility) report every minute, automatic StVO switching, no minimum duration, operators can switch manually. Sequences like 80-60-100 occur because speed is cut only where needed. [P, publisher not stated in the file] — [Funktionsweise VBA, Salzburgwiki](https://wiki.sn.at/wiki/images/d/d0/Tempo_80_Funktionsweise_VBA.pdf)
- ASFINAG brochure: Austria-wide telematics "will" reduce accidents by up to 35%, injury accidents up to 31%, serious injuries up to 30%. These are forecasts for the whole telematics programme, not weather-specific measurements. It also says section-control sites can reduce the limit when sensors detect ice or water film. [P] — [ASFINAG 2007, "Intelligent leiten"](https://www.vol.at/2007/04/asfinag_Verkehrsbeeinflussungsanlagen_Oesterreich.pdf)

### Inferences
- The A16 finding sets a design constraint: a displayed limit can raise speeds when conditions are worse than the display assumes, so very low visibility needs a lower value or a warning rather than a fixed floor.
- Germany appears to automate wet and visibility switching but keeps ice as a human decision, which suggests operators do not trust automated ice detection enough for automatic limits.

### Gaps
- I could not obtain MARZ or the FGSV 2010 Hinweise, so no German mapping of wetness, visibility or ice class to a limit is verified. The FGSV Wissenspapier (2019) was not read either.
- No Austrian RVS switching criteria, no independent evaluation of weather-triggered VBA limits. The TU Graz impact analysis poster on VBA Tirol found in search addresses traffic flow, and I could not read its results.
- The A16 accident evaluation (Stoop, TU Delft, 1994) was not read.

## Typical limit values under ice, black ice and low visibility

### Takeaway
On roads with a base of about 100 km/h, deployed regulatory limits under ice or snow cluster at 80 km/h for moderate slipperiness and 60 km/h (occasionally 70) as the floor for very poor conditions. Low visibility is typically 80 km/h below about 150-200 m and 60 km/h below about 70-100 m. US freeways stop at 30-35 mph (48-56 km/h), then close.

[P] — [Finnra 54/2003](https://www.tieh.fi/julkaisut/pdf/3200841_vms-turva.pdf). In the Finnish policy view, "the highest speed limit should only be used cautiously and the lowest to be on the safe side". [P] — [Schirokoff et al. 2006](https://sirwec.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Torino-D-22.pdf)

### Inferences
- Black ice as a named condition appears in only one document I read (Central Finland, autumn, 80 km/h on a 100 km/h road). Elsewhere it falls under "ice", usually 60-80 km/h on a 100 km/h base, or roughly 25-40 mph on physics-based US logic.
- Physics-based values for ice (CDOT, 25-35 mph) are far below deployed European values (60-80 km/h). Operators appear to set limits drivers will accept, not limits that preserve dry-road stopping distance.
- **Transfer to an 80 km/h road:** direct evidence is thin. The closest crash evidence is Sweden's 90 to 70 km/h trial (cars -4 km/h, injury accidents 33.6 to 25 in one winter). The Finnish 80 to 60 km/h and 80 to 50 km/h systems have no published effect data that I found. US results come from 65-75 mph freeways with high truck shares (50-60% at Elk Mountain), separated carriageways and closures as a fallback; none of this applies to an 80 km/h two-lane road. Nordic tyre fitment (studded and winter tyres, named as a factor by Peltola 2000) also limits transfer to places without them.

### Gaps
- I found no measured crash effect of a limit reduction specifically under black ice. The reported effects are on winter periods or all weather-activated periods, not by ice state.

## Limits reduced for darkness alone

### Takeaway
Yes, but rarely, and the two clear weather-system cases were dropped. Finnish systems briefly lowered limits to 80 km/h for darkness and abandoned the rule. Dusk-to-dawn limits exist for wildlife, with small speed effects. Static statutory night limits exist in Montana. I found no evaluation of a darkness-only limit showing a crash reduction in general traffic.

### Cited Findings
- Finland road 1 (Salo section): in the first winter of operation the limit was lowered to 80 km/h for darkness; "this principle was later abandoned". [P] — [Finnra 54/2003](https://www.tieh.fi/julkaisut/pdf/3200841_vms-turva.pdf). The same report's App. 2 lists "dark and twilight" as a Class C condition for that road.
- Finland road 9 (Tampere-Orivesi): initially lowered to 80 km/h for darkness and abandoned; control rules moved to the weather-road model with automatic control in autumn 2001. [P] — [Finnra 54/2003](https://www.tieh.fi/julkaisut/pdf/3200841_vms-turva.pdf). No reason for abandonment or effect is given.
- By 2003 Finnish systems were not controlled by light level, but the authors said accident risk was many times higher at night, that lowering two-lane roads to 80 km/h in winter darkness "could be justified" even in good conditions, and that 70% (road 1) and about 50% (road 9) of interviewed drivers found 80 km/h or lower suitable in good winter conditions in the dark. A recommendation, not a tested measure. [P] — [Finnra 54/2003](https://www.tieh.fi/julkaisut/pdf/3200841_vms-turva.pdf)
- Central Finland rule combines darkness with weather: "heavy rain in darkness" gives 80 km/h. Also, a 100 to 80 change made during darkness on a wet surface is not reversed until after darkness ends. [P] — [Finnra 54/2003, App. 3](https://www.tieh.fi/julkaisut/pdf/3200841_vms-turva.pdf)
- Finnish seasonal limits are officially named "winter and dark-season" limits, and darkness is one stated justification, but they apply day and night. [P press release] — [Pohjois-Suomen elinvoimakeskus 2026](https://www.sttinfo.fi/tiedote/72447134/talvi-ja-pimean-ajan-nopeusrajoitukset-tulevat-voimaan-talla-viikolla?lang=fi); [Peltola 2000 record](https://cris.vtt.fi/en/publications/seasonally-changing-speed-limits-effects-on-speeds-and-accidents/) [A]
- Netherlands A16 had a "night correction" to the fog thresholds, removed on 29 January 1992 because the morning switch-off suddenly changed signs without any change in visibility, which confused drivers. It modified fog thresholds and was not a darkness limit as such. [P] — [TNO-TM 1994 C-48](https://open.rijkswaterstaat.nl/publish/pages/153378/70142.pdf)
- Wyoming experiment on two-lane highways: 70 to 55 mph (113 to 89 km/h) dusk to dawn in deer seasons at six sites, with flashing-beacon signs and comparison stretches. Drivers slowed only 3-5 mph against a 15 mph limit change. Night speeds at LaBarge were 60.9 mph under the 55 mph limit against 65.7-66.2 under 70 mph. No evidence of fewer wildlife collisions at winter sites. At migration sites, crashes per mile were 37% lower (p=0.03) and carcasses 31% lower (p=0.10), but the authors did not attribute this to the limit. Recommendation: not effective unless other measures change speeds. [P] — [Riginos et al. 2019, FHWA-WY-1904F](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/42488/dot_42488_DS1.pdf)
- A Colorado study of night limits found no evidence drivers slowed, and collision changes ranged from large decreases to large increases. [S, in Riginos et al. 2019]
- CDOT guidelines list "locations that warrant reduced nighttime speed limits" as a possible VSL use, handled by standard operating procedures. No threshold or example given. [P] — [CDOT 2023](https://www.codot.gov/safety/traffic-safety/assets/variable-speed-limits/cdot-variable-speed-limits-guidelines_2023-11-02.pdf)
- Montana statutory limits: 70 mph day and 65 mph night on public highways. [X] — [MDT slide deck](https://mdt.mt.gov/visionzero/plans/docs/chsp/2023/K-Speed-Studies-Limits-Relph-Final.pdf). Other sources conflict on the interstate night limit.
- Virginia I-77: night crashes CMF 0.87, not significant, on a corridor whose VSL targets fog. [P] — [Avelar et al. 2021](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/57267/dot_57267_DS1.pdf)

### Inferences
- Darkness-only reductions have a weak record: driver response is small (Wyoming about 0.2-0.33 mph per 1 mph posted) and two Finnish systems dropped them, for reasons the report does not give. A plausible explanation, not tested in anything I read, is that drivers cannot perceive a reason for the limit on a dry, clear night, unlike snow or fog.
- A darkness rule is more defensible as a combination with wet or slippery conditions, which is how Central Finland used it.

### Gaps
- The Wyoming study is about wildlife collisions, so it does not measure general crash effects. I found no controlled study of darkness-only limits on all crashes. I could not confirm whether Texas previously had a night limit that was repealed.

## Compliance and negative findings

### Takeaway
Drivers respond to only part of a posted reduction, typically one-fifth to three-quarters of it depending on context. Documented null or adverse findings exist in every programme with a real evaluation, so a positive expectation should not be assumed.

### Cited Findings
- Posted-versus-observed response: Finland 3.4-3.8 km/h per 20 km/h [A]; Wyoming winter 0.47-0.75 mph per mph [A]; Wyoming night 3-5 mph per 15 mph [P]; Sweden about 4 km/h per 20 km/h [P]. Sources as cited above.
- Wyoming compliance worsens with size of the reduction: 5.4, 8.5 and 13 mph above posted for ≤15, 16-25 and ≥30 mph reductions. [P] — [Sui and Young 2013](https://www.westernite.org/annualmeetings/13_Phoenix/Papers/Session%205D%20-%20Sui.pdf)
- Finland rural seasonal and VSL drivers: recall of displayed limit 86-95% and 95% finding systems good and necessary. [S, in Finnra 54/2003, citing Hautala et al. 2001 and Rämä et al. 1999] — [Finnra 54/2003](https://www.tieh.fi/julkaisut/pdf/3200841_vms-turva.pdf)
- Injury risk went up on Finnish roads 4 and 9: +8% winter and +21% summer (non-significant). [P] — [Finnra 54/2003](https://www.tieh.fi/julkaisut/pdf/3200841_vms-turva.pdf)
- A16: higher speeds below 35 m. [P] — [TNO 1994](https://open.rijkswaterstaat.nl/publish/pages/153378/70142.pdf)
- Sweden 110 to 90 km/h: injury accidents +20.9% (significant by the report's test; one winter, non-random selection). [P] — [VTI notat 58-2000](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:669972/FULLTEXT01.pdf)
- Virginia I-77: no detectable change. [P] — [Avelar et al. 2021](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/57267/dot_57267_DS1.pdf)
- Variance increases: Washington westbound [X]; simulator [P]. Saudi Arabia fog advisory of 40 km/h: mean speed -6.5 km/h but variability not reduced and advisory exceeded. [S, in SPR 743, citing Al-Ghamdi 2007] — [SPR 743 Phase 1](https://westerntransportationinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/03/4W3603_SPR743_VSL_System.pdf)
- M25 fog signals: net mean speed reduction about 3 km/h. [S, in Rämä 2001] — [VTT Publications 447](https://sarjaweb.vtt.fi/pdf/publications/2001/P447.pdf)
- Operational failure: Nevada blank and inconsistent signs. [P] — [FHWA-HOP-17-003](https://ops.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/fhwahop17003/ch3.htm)

**Study design ranking of the crash evidence read:**
- Controlled comparison, no significance: Finnra 54/2003; SIRWEC 2006.
- Controlled comparison, one winter: VTI notat 58-2000.
- Interrupted time series with covariates, no comparison group: FHWA-HRT-21-053 (Wyoming, Virginia).
- Regression with weather and traffic: Saha et al. 2015.
- Uncontrolled before-after: Ohio (all figures), Utah amber signs, WYDOT naive, Germany in Robinson 2000, Austria brochure (forecast), Tennessee and South Carolina fog anecdotes.

### Inferences
- Regression to the mean is a live risk: most systems were sited after bad crash histories or a triggering pileup (Ohio's December 2016 crash), and none of the before-after studies I read adjusts for it.
- Speed variance claims are less consistent than mean-speed claims. It dropped in Finland, the A16 (slightly) and Wyoming, but was mixed or worse in Washington.

### Gaps
- No study I read reports compliance specifically on an 80 km/h rural road during ice.

## Stated or validated targets such as a maximum crash rate

### Takeaway
I found no deployment that states or validates a maximum acceptable crash rate. Limits are set from sensor classes, look-up tables, operator judgement or stopping-distance physics, and crash outcomes are only evaluated afterwards. Crash statistics are used to decide where to install, not what limit to display.

### Cited Findings
- Colorado uses Safety Performance Functions and Level of Service of Safety (LOSS) to establish that a corridor is a VSL candidate, for example I-25 sitting above a baseline around SPF percentile 0.55. The displayed limit then comes from friction, grade and visibility calculations. [P] — [CDOT 2023](https://www.codot.gov/safety/traffic-safety/assets/variable-speed-limits/cdot-variable-speed-limits-guidelines_2023-11-02.pdf)
- CDOT's design target is a constant stopping distance (188 ft at 65 mph). This is the closest thing to a stated safety target, and it is a physical surrogate. [P] — [CDOT 2023](https://www.codot.gov/safety/traffic-safety/assets/variable-speed-limits/cdot-variable-speed-limits-guidelines_2023-11-02.pdf)
- The A16 evaluation compares observed speeds with a stopping-within-visibility curve, again a surrogate, and not a crash-rate target. [P] — [TNO 1994](https://open.rijkswaterstaat.nl/publish/pages/153378/70142.pdf)
- Finnish policy is qualitative: the highest limit used cautiously, the lowest on the safe side, control based on automated reliable classification. [P] — [Schirokoff et al. 2006](https://sirwec.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Torino-D-22.pdf)
- Sweden's inventory screens sites for crashes above what weather would predict, a siting method. [P/X: method described in the report; the phrasing about excess accidents was seen in a search excerpt] — [Trafikverket 2012:153](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:1363816/FULLTEXT01.pdf)
- WYDOT sets a closure rule at below 35 mph, an operational floor with no crash-rate basis stated. [P] — [Sui and Young 2013](https://www.westernite.org/annualmeetings/13_Phoenix/Papers/Session%205D%20-%20Sui.pdf)
- FHWA notes that setting limits consistently is hard because "supporting data may not provide for clear recommendations", and that benefits vary by site. [P] — [FHWA deck, Aug 2024](https://tmcpfs.ops.fhwa.dot.gov/pdfs/TMS_Managing_Use_VSLs_Adverse_Weather.pdf)

### Inferences
- A speed advisor that claims to meet a maximum crash rate would be going beyond what these operators claim. The defensible framing from this literature is condition classes plus a stopping-distance check, with crash effects reported as uncertain ranges.
- Published per-condition risk multipliers (Finland, nine times on snow and 20 times on ice) are approximate and depend on limited exposure data, so a crash-rate target built on them would carry wide uncertainty.

### Gaps
- Absence is based on the documents I could read. Internal operating procedures (WSDOT 2024 guidelines, MARZ, ASFINAG and Trafikverket switching rules) are unpublished or were unavailable and could contain targets.
