# Speed limits as an air-quality measure, and the applicable PM10 and NO2 thresholds

Notes compiled 9 November 2026 for an advisory speed recommendation on an 80 km/h road (candidate limits 40, 50, 60, 70, 80 km/h). All concentrations are in µg/m³ unless stated.

Evidence tags on each figure:
- **[READ]**: I read the figure in the text of the primary document.
- **[READ-X]**: primary document, read through an automated page extractor (EUR-Lex, RIS and repository pages). The figure is from the primary source, but I did not see the line myself.
- **[ABSTRACT]**: I read only the abstract or bibliographic record. The full text was not accessible.
- **[QUOTED]**: seen only in a search snippet or another source. Treat as unverified.
- **[DERIVED]**: my own arithmetic on figures from the named source.

## What are the legal and guideline thresholds for PM10 and NO2?

### Takeaway
The EU limits change in two steps: values to be attained by 11 December 2026 stay at the 2008/50/EC levels (PM10 40 annual, 50 daily with 35 exceedances; NO2 40 annual, 200 hourly with 18 exceedances), and from 1 January 2030 they tighten to PM10 20 annual, 45 daily with 18 exceedances, and NO2 20 annual, 50 daily, 200 hourly with 3 exceedances. Austria's IG-L is stricter than the current EU values on the NO2 annual mean (30, with a 5 margin in the text), PM10 exceedance days (25) and the NO2 short-term averaging period (half-hour).

### Inferences
- For code, store thresholds as dated, jurisdiction-tagged records with averaging period and allowed exceedances. A single "NO2 limit" number would be wrong on every axis.
- An Austrian deployment needs the 30 (or 35 with margin) annual NO2 check and 25 PM10 days today. EU-only logic would understate exceedances.
- A speed advisory acting on hourly or half-hourly data can be compared with the short-term values. Annual limits can only be tracked as a running mean or projection, not "exceeded" in real time. The 2030 NO2 daily limit of 50 (18 exceedances) is a new and more operationally relevant target for such a system.

### Gaps
- I did not confirm whether the Austrian minister dropped the NO2 5 margin by regulation, so the operative annual value could be 30 or 35. The RIS list showed BGBl. I Nr. 73/2018 as the latest amendment.
- I did not find an Austrian transposition of 2024/2881. With the deadline of 11 December 2026 still a month away, IG-L values may change shortly.
- The publication date, and so the entry-into-force date, of 2024/2881 was not in the text I read.
- The extractor described Annex I Table 2 of 2024/2881 as applying to 11 December 2026; "to be attained by" is my wording, so check the table header before quoting it.

## Austria: what triggers IG-L "Lufthunderter" limits, and what were the measured effects?

### Takeaway
The best Austrian evidence is for NO2 and NOx. Permanent 100 km/h on the Tyrol A12 lowered modelled NOx emissions by about 11-13% and annual NO2 by 3-6 µg/m³ against 130/110. A 100 to 80 km/h trial on the A1 at Salzburg, the closest analogue here, gave -5.6% NOx emissions with only 70% of the theoretically expected effect and -3 to -4 µg/m³ NO2 at 5 m from the carriageway. PM10 evidence is thin.

### Cited Findings
- Legal timeline for Tyrol: winter 100 km/h in 2006; immission-dependent limit from 6 November 2007 (LGBl. 72/2007, based on § 14 IG-L); permanent 100 km/h from 20 November 2014 (LGBl. 145/2014) [READ-X]. — [Land Tirol, Beschränkungen nach IG-Luft](https://www.tirol.gv.at/umwelt/umweltrecht/luftreinhalterecht/100-kmh-luft)
- The ordinance responds to CJEU case C-28/09, in which a permanent speed limit was named as a milder measure to exhaust before a sectoral truck ban [READ-X]. — [Land Tirol](https://www.tirol.gv.at/umwelt/umweltrecht/luftreinhalterecht/100-kmh-luft)
- The flexible limit was software-controlled according to pollution and traffic, and optimised for NO2 [READ]. Its annual switched-on share would have been Vomp 29.1%, Kundl 24.1%, Imst 32.7% in 2014/15 [READ]. — [Thudium and Chélala, Oekoscience, 15.12.2015](https://www.tirol.gv.at/fileadmin/buergerservice/kundmachungen/umweltschutz/ALuft/160120_thu_Be_T100PermanentTirol2015finalV2.pdf)

- A12 Imst, emission-side study: 100 km/h all day versus 130 cut NOx by 15% for cars and light vans and exhaust PM10 by about 18%, total PM10 by 8%. Against all traffic the cuts were -7% NOx, -13% exhaust PM10 and -6% total PM10. Measured mean speed under T130 was 117 km/h, not 130 [READ]. HBEFA 2.1-era fleet, so these do not represent today's cars. — [TU Graz IVT, 2006, Evaluierung A12 Imst](https://www.tirol.gv.at/fileadmin/buergerservice/kundmachungen/umweltschutz/000Evaluierung_der_emissionsseitigen_Auswirkungen.pdf)
- A12 Imst, 2022: maximum half-hour NO2 was 110 µg/m³, and the scenario value with T130 is about 20% higher (about 132). Since an ordinance of 28.01.2021 the limit applies only in November-January. The author considers the 200 limit very unlikely to be reached even without T100 [READ]. — [Thudium, 01.07.2023](https://www.tirol.gv.at/fileadmin/themen/umwelt/luftqualitaet/StellungnahmeAufhebungT100Winter.pdf)
- Salzburg A1, fixed T80 trial, 20.2.-19.5.2014 on 10.3 km, versus T100 phase 17.10.2013-19.2.2014 [READ]: light-duty mean speed fell 12 km/h (94 to 82), heavy traffic 86 to 80. The decline is attributed to a smaller speed difference (8 versus about 11 km/h) and lower baseline NO2 (about 30 versus 40) [READ]. — [Thudium and Chélala, 19.08.2022](http://www.salzburg.gv.at/fileadmin/SP-Dateien/umweltnaturwasser_/Documents/Umwelt/Be_EvalT80_SiezenheimA1_2021_2022.pdf)
- Styria, PM10-driven: the 100 km/h motorway limit from 15 December 2006 to 14 March 2007 is credited with saving 1.5 t PM10, about 2,000 t CO2 and 16.5 t NOx. The Land also claims 30-50% fewer exceedance days depending on station [READ]. — [Land Steiermark, Erläuterungen zur IG-L-Maßnahmenverordnung](https://www.verwaltung.steiermark.at/cms/dokumente/11682709_74837683/f3edf97e/IGL-Ma%C3%9Fnahmenverordnung_Erl%C3%A4uterungen.pdf)

### Inferences
- The speed change actually achieved drives the outcome. At Salzburg a 20 km/h limit cut delivered a 12 km/h speed cut (light vehicles), and later only 8 km/h. An advisory model should use realised speed, not the posted limit.
- The air-quality yield of a given speed cut shrinks as the fleet cleans up: 1.8 to about 1 µg/m³ at the same site within a few years, and Tyrol has already rolled back part of its limit. Static effect percentages will overstate today's benefit.
- The 30-50% exceedance-day claim from Styria cannot be attributed to the speed limit. It compares winters with no meteorological control, and 1.5 t of PM10 over a whole region is very small. I would not use it.
- Heavy vehicles were outside the Austrian limit changes, so these studies do not show what happens when trucks are slowed.

### Gaps
- I could not verify the numeric switching thresholds of the Tyrol and Salzburg algorithms. The reports I read describe them only qualitatively.
- A search summary said Styria has applied a temporary, PM10-triggered 100 km/h on parts of the A2 and A9 since 2009, but I could not tie that statement to a specific document, so treat it as unverified. I also found no quantified evaluation of that scheme.
- Tyrol and Salzburg evaluations cover NOx and NO2 only. I found no PM10 evaluation for them.
- I found no peer-reviewed evaluation of the Austrian schemes. All reports are commissioned by the Länder from one consultancy (Oekoscience) or TU Graz, and they are model-assisted.
- The Land Tirol NO2 programme evaluation mentions the limit only in passing, by keyword search of its text. [Evaluierung NO2-Maßnahmenprogramm](https://www.tirol.gv.at/fileadmin/themen/umwelt/luftqualitaet/EVALUIERUNG_NO2-MASSNAHMENPROGRAMM.pdf)

## Netherlands: what did the 80 km/h zones and the 2020 daytime 100 km/h limit achieve?

### Takeaway
Strictly enforced 100 to 80 km/h zones cut NOx emissions by 5-30% and PM10 emissions by 5-25%, mostly by smoothing traffic, and lowered near-road annual NO2 by about 2-4 µg/m³ and PM10 by about 0.5-2 µg/m³. The 2020 national limit is estimated, not measured, to have cut car and van NOx by 6-9%.

### Cited Findings
- Keuken et al. 2010: 80 km/h zones on urban motorways with camera enforcement over 2-4 km (Rotterdam, Amsterdam): emission reductions of 5-30% for NOx and 5-25% for PM10. The size depends mainly on the share of congested traffic before and after. Benefits were largest with a high share of heavy vehicles. Combined measurement and modelling [ABSTRACT]. — [Keuken, Jonkers, Wilmink, Wesseling, Sci Total Environ 408(12), 2517-2526, 2010, DOI 10.1016/j.scitotenv.2010.03.008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scitotenv.2010.03.008)
- Dijkema et al. 2008: Amsterdam ring, 100 to 80 km/h, adjacent station, first year versus prior year, adjusted for flow, congestion and downwind exposure: traffic contribution fell by PM10 2.20 µg/m³ (95% CI 1.43-2.98), PM1 0.42 (0.01-0.82), Black Smoke 3.57 (1.50-5.65). An unchanged comparison section also fell (PM10 0.97, Black Smoke 2.43). PM10 and PM1 reductions were significantly greater at the intervention site [ABSTRACT]. — [Dijkema, van der Zee, Brunekreef, van Strien, Atmos Environ, 2008, pp. 9098-9105](https://manuscript.isc.ac/Inventory/10/761511.htm)
- A13 Overschie (80 km/h from May 2002; TNO 2003 measurements and modelling): traffic NOx emissions -15 to -25%, PM10 emissions -25 to -35% at constant traffic [READ]. Annual NO2 fell by 2-4 and PM10 by 0.5-1 between 50 and 200 m. Table 1 of the memo gives NO2 58.7 to 54.4 (-4.3) at 50 m and 46.2 to 44.6 (-1.6) at 200 m; PM10 34.1 to 32.7 (-1.4) at 50 m and 31.1 to 30.7 (-0.4) at 200 m. These are calculated annual means. — [Rijkswaterstaat DVS, Verhoging maximumsnelheid 80 km zones naar 100 km/h, November 2011](https://open.rijkswaterstaat.nl/publish/pages/7841/16-verhoging_maximumsnelheid_80-km_zones_naar_100_kmh_effecten_op_luchtkwaliteit_rijkswaterstaat.pdf)
- Evaluation of the zones introduced November 2005, NOx emission reduction attributed to 80 km/h: A10 West about 30%, A12 Voorburg 20% (the memo's sentence names the "A13" here, see Gaps), A20 Rotterdam 30%, A12 Utrecht about 30% [READ]. ### Inferences
- The Dutch zones bundled a lower limit with section control. The reported reductions are largely a flow-smoothing and enforcement effect, so a posted limit alone, or an advisory that is not enforced, should be expected to deliver less.
- PM10 from the motorway was only about 6-14% of total concentration at the sampled hotspots, so even a 25% cut in road PM10 is roughly 0.5-1 µg/m³ of total. This caps what a speed measure can do for PM10 compliance.
- Dijkema's control section fell too. A naive difference suggests a net PM10 effect closer to 1.2 than 2.2 µg/m³ [DERIVED]; the paper's own adjusted comparison, which I did not read in full, should take precedence.

### Gaps
- I could not access the 2007 "Evaluatie 80 km zones" report or the TNO original; those figures are quoted through the 2011 memo. Its Table 1 is labelled 2006 while the section is about 2003, and section 4.1 cites "A13" while describing A12 Voorburg. These look like labelling errors, but I could not check them against the originals, so the Voorburg 20% in particular is uncertain.
- SWOV's report on Dutch speed-limit experience was blocked by bot protection. A claimed "up to 5%" air-quality improvement from 100 to 80 km/h is unverified. [SWOV record](https://swov.nl/nl/publicatie/reducing-speed-limits-highways-dutch-experiences-and-impact-air-pollution-noise-level)
- No before/after concentration measurement of the 2020 limit was found. PM10 is not addressed.

## Spain: what do the Barcelona 80 km/h and Madrid M-30 evaluations show, and why do they conflict?

### Takeaway
Barcelona is a real conflict: models show small improvements (up to 5.7% NO2, 3.0% PM10), while a difference-in-differences study of station data found the 80 km/h limit slightly raised NOx and PM10, and a variable-speed system clearly lowered them. Madrid's 90 to 70 km/h M-30 case reports -16.4% NOx but is confounded by a road redesign.

### Cited Findings
- Gonçalves et al. 2008 (model only: WRF-ARW/HERMES/CMAQ; one summer working day of a 2004 photochemical episode): an 80 km/h cap across the metropolitan area lowered local NO2, SO2 and PM10 by up to about 5.7%, 5.3% and 3.0% within the affected area. Ozone rose up to about 2.4% in the VOC-limited urban area, and effects in the urban plume were negligible. Results are also sensitive to whether modelled traffic speed is constant or hourly variable [ABSTRACT]. — [Gonçalves, Jiménez-Guerrero, López, Baldasano, Atmos Environ, 2008, pp. 8389-8402](https://manuscript.isc.ac/Inventory/10/761446.htm)
- Baldasano et al. 2010 (model): "enhancements in air quality levels (5-7%)" of primary pollutants over the area, benefiting 1.35 million people [QUOTED]. A Spanish press report quoted Baldasano giving 14% overall on affected roads under a headline of 11% [QUOTED]. — [Baldasano, Gonçalves, Soret, Jiménez-Guerrero, Atmos Environ 44(25), 2997-3006, 2010, DOI 10.1016/j.atmosenv.2010.05.013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.atmosenv.2010.05.013)
- Bel and Rosell 2013 (difference-in-differences on station data; Catalonia cut limits to 80 km/h in 2008 on 19 municipalities, previously 63.2% at 100 km/h and 20.4% at 120 km/h; lifted February 2011) [READ, accepted manuscript]:
  - 80 km/h limit: NOx +1.7 to +3.2%, PM10 +5.3 to +5.9%.
  - Variable speed system (2009): NOx -7.7 to -17.1%, PM10 -14.5 to -17.3%. Omitting August roughly halves the NOx effect for some specifications (for example -17.1% becomes -9.3%).
  - — [Bel, Rosell, Transp Res Part D 23, 90-97, 2013, DOI 10.1016/j.trd.2013.04.005, open postprint](https://hdl.handle.net/2445/44528)
- Bel, Bolancé, Guillén, Rosell 2015 (quantile regression): reducing 120/100 to 80 km/h had no effect, or a slightly increasing effect in some pollution scenarios, on either pollutant. Variable speed improved air quality for both [ABSTRACT]. — [Transp Res Part D 36, 76-85, 2015, DOI 10.1016/j.trd.2015.02.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.trd.2015.02.003)
- Perez-Prada and Monzon 2017: planned redesign of Madrid's inner ring road; the rebuilt south-west section went from 90 to 70 km/h, mainly for safety: CO2 -14.4%, NOx -16.4% in the section, overall travel time about constant, saturation slightly lower [ABSTRACT]. No PM10. — [J Transp Geogr 58, 256-268, 2017, DOI 10.1016/j.jtrangeo.2016.12.018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jtrangeo.2016.12.018)
- Madrid has applied the same 90 to 70 km/h cut on the M-30 during NO2 episodes [QUOTED]. — [La Marea, November 2015](https://lamarea.com/2015/11/12/madrid-supera-54-veces-en-dos-dias-el-limite-de-no2-permitido)

### Inferences
- The conflict is largely methodological. Models apply a speed-emission curve and assume drivers comply, so they cannot show adverse effects from congestion, compliance or displaced traffic. The observational study can show those, but on roads that were already congested, where the limit may not change actual speeds much or may disturb flow.
- Bel and Rosell's result is not proof that lower speeds raise emissions per kilometre. Their limit applied to congested urban motorways and the sign may reflect flow effects and unobserved confounding. Their +5% PM10 is implausible as a pure speed effect and should be read as "no detectable benefit".
- Variable speed outperforming a fixed limit is the most decision-relevant Spanish finding: dynamic, flow-aware operation is what yields the benefit. That supports an advisory system, but the variable-speed roads were also selected, so it is not clean causal proof.
- Bel and Rosell describe Keuken et al. 2010 as finding "no significant impact", which does not match Keuken's abstract (5-30% NOx, 5-25% PM10). Do not repeat that characterisation.

### Gaps
- I could not read the Perez-Prada and Monzon full text (HTTP 403), so I cannot say how much of the -16.4% is speed versus redesign or how it was computed. The reported effect is not a measured concentration change.
- The full texts of Gonçalves 2008 and Baldasano 2010 were not accessible. A 2013 Madrid City Council study reportedly found about 0.7% of M-30 emissions for a cut to 80 km/h, but the table was hard to read in the snippet [QUOTED]. [Estudio reducción tráfico Madrid 2013](https://airedemadrid.madrid.es/UnidadesDescentralizadas/Sostenibilidad/CalidadAire/Publicaciones/Estudiosambientales/Estudio_reduccion_trafico_Madrid_2013.pdf)

## Switzerland and other European evaluations: what was found?

### Takeaway
A national 80 km/h motorway limit in Swiss modelling cut NOx by only about 4% and peak ozone by under 1%. An emergency 80 km/h week in Ticino shows large calculated light-vehicle reductions but only 1-3% measured PM10 change. Most PM10 in that episode was secondary.

### Cited Findings
- Keller et al. (PSI report, 2005): MM5/CAMx modelling of a 4-day period in August 2003, Swiss motorways to 80 km/h: NOx emissions about -4%, VOC not significantly affected, peak ozone change under 1% [READ-X, abstract record]. — [Keller, Andreani-Aksoyoglu, Tinguely, Prévôt, PSI, ETDE-CH-0501, OSTI 20671694](https://www.osti.gov/etdeweb/biblio/20671694)
- Keller et al. 2008, journal version: compares 120 km/h reference with an 80 km/h cap. One later summary gives NOx -4.3% in summer, NMVOC about constant, ozone under 1% (under 1 ppb) [QUOTED]. Authors judged temporary regional measures weak when the achievable emission cut is below 50% [QUOTED]. — [Env Modelling and Software 23(3), 322-332, 2008](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1364815207000825)
- Ticino A2 emergency limit, 25.2-2.3.2008, triggered by PM10 above 100 µg/m³ at 5 stations, lifted when PM10 returned below 50 [READ]. Light-vehicle emission factors from MICET 2.1 (FOEN, 2004): NOx 0.323 g/km at 120, 0.269 at 100, 0.208 at 80 km/h; PM10 0.008, 0.006, 0.004 g/km. Those are 36% (NOx) and 50% (PM10) lower at 80 than at 120 km/h. Heavy vehicles were excluded because they were already limited to 80 km/h. — [Canton Ticino SPAAS, April 2008](https://m4.ti.ch/fileadmin/DT/temi/oasi/documenti/Rapporto_introduzione_80kmh_2008.pdf)
- Measured changes, week before versus week during, no weather control [READ]: PM10 at motorway stations -1 to -3%, and away from the motorway +6 to +7%. NOx weekly mean along the motorway -20 to -25%, with daily peaks at Bioggio down 20% in the morning to almost 50% in the evening, and no difference at Pregassona, away from the motorway. The report puts secondary PM10 at an estimated 60-70% in winter. — [Canton Ticino 2008](https://m4.ti.ch/fileadmin/DT/temi/oasi/documenti/Rapporto_introduzione_80kmh_2008.pdf)
- A temporary 2003 limit on some Swiss motorways was judged ineffective at reducing ozone [QUOTED]. — [Swissinfo](https://www.swissinfo.ch/eng/speed-limits-fail-to-reduce-ozone-levels/969770)
- Int Panis et al. 2011: for passenger cars both of their methods indicate only minor changes in NOx and CO2; results are mixed for PM; lower truck maximum speeds consistently lower CO2 and fuel use [QUOTED, abstract snippet]. — [Transport Policy 18(1), 32-37, 2011](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:trapol:v:18:y:2011:i:1:p:32-37)

### Inferences
- Keller's 4% is network-wide across Switzerland. Near-road Dutch and Austrian figures of 5-30% are road-specific. The two are not in conflict.
- The Ticino NOx signal is only a week of data across a foehn event the report itself cites as ending the episode, so it is suggestive at most.
- High-PM10 winter episodes are dominated by secondary and regional aerosol, so a local speed limit triggered by PM10 is targeting a pollutant it can barely influence. NOx is the more defensible trigger, and it also reduces secondary nitrate only at regional scale.

### Gaps
- I could not access the Keller 2008 journal text (HTTP 403), and its PM10 result is unverified. One secondary summary says "no significant change in PM10", but I could not confirm that.
- The Hasselt repository for Int Panis returned HTTP 503.

## At which speeds are NOx and PM10 emissions lowest, and does going below about 70 km/h still help?

### Takeaway
I could not obtain the HBEFA tables or the COPERT coefficient file, so I cannot give a verified curve or speed of minimum. The one road-type table I could read (TNO, pre-RDE Euro 6 diesel cars) has its lowest NOx at rural conditions, not at the lowest speeds, which is consistent with a U-shape but does not establish one. There is no verified evidence that cutting a free-flowing limit below about 70 km/h continues to reduce NOx, and for heavy vehicles SCR performance gives a mechanism for an increase at low load.

### Cited Findings
- COPERT/EMEP form: EF = (Alpha x V² + Beta x V + Gamma + Delta / V) / (Epsilon x V² + Zeta x V + Eta) x (1 - RF), with coefficients only in an Appendix 4 electronic annex [READ]. — [EMEP/EEA Guidebook 2023, Update 2025, chapter 1.A.3.b.i-iv, Eq. 25, COPERT 5.9](https://copert.emisia.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/1.A.3.b.i-iv-Road-transport-2025.pdf)
- Guidebook usage rules, critical for this system [READ]: factors apply only within tabulated speed ranges and extrapolation is "not advisable"; use only with average travelling speed, not spot or constant speeds; they represent constant-speed performance only above 100 km/h; they should not be applied where the driving pattern differs substantially from the norm, for example traffic calming. — [EMEP/EEA Guidebook, section 4.5.1](https://copert.emisia.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/1.A.3.b.i-iv-Road-transport-2025.pdf)
- Heavy-duty diesel speed-dependent factors in COPERT come from HBEFA [READ]. — [EMEP/EEA Guidebook, Update 2025](https://copert.emisia.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/1.A.3.b.i-iv-Road-transport-2025.pdf)
- Euro 6 diesel car NOx/NO2 (mg/km) in Dutch TNO VERSIT+ factors (update 2014), reproduced by TU Graz: urban congested 379/119; urban normal 237/71; urban free-flow 252/69; rural normal 202/53; motorway 80 km/h 260/76; motorway 100 km/h 394/123; motorway 120 km/h 437/131; motorway 130 km/h 468/138; motorway congested 377/132 [READ]. Pre-RDE Euro 6. — [TU Graz, Update of Emission Factors for EURO 5 and EURO 6 vehicles for HBEFA 3.2, Table 11, 2013](https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/6207922a2acc01004530a67e/625e7ef73f2c1259c56737b4_HBEFA32_EF_Euro_5_6_TUG.pdf)
- Newer real-world performance: six Euro 6d-temp diesel cars averaged about 44 mg/km hot NOx across the HBEFA traffic mix, roughly 5 to 10 times below the pre-RDE values above, although a traffic-mix average and road-type values are not strictly comparable [READ-X; ratio DERIVED]. — [Weller et al., Frontiers in Mechanical Engineering 5:45, 2019, DOI 10.3389/fmech.2019.00045](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmech.2019.00045)

- A 2024 study reports remote-sensing NOx curves exceeding COPERT for Euro VI at ratios up to 18.6 at 80 km/h and 24.6 at 110 km/h, and above COPERT for Euro 6d across the whole speed range [QUOTED]. I could not read it, so authors and journal are unverified. — [ScienceDirect S2212095524003006](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2212095524003006)
- Euro 6 diesel PEMS (39 cars): NOx averaged 1.6 times COPERT and NO2 2.5 times [QUOTED]. — [O'Driscoll et al., Atmos Environ 2016](https://spiral.imperial.ac.uk/handle/10044/1/40122)
- City of London 20 mph study (Imperial College, 2013, Euro IV cars, modelled drive cycles): petrol NOx +7.9%, PM10 -8.3%, CO2 +2.1%; diesel NOx -8.2%, PM10 -8.3%, CO2 -0.9%. The report notes that average-speed models suggest higher emissions at lower urban limits [QUOTED]. — [City of London emissions report](https://www.edinburgh.gov.uk/downloads/file/25178/city-of-london-emissions-report)
- Exhaust PM from modern diesels: Euro VI PM factors are "close to zero" due to DPFs, and DPFs reduce PM and PN effectively in all driving situations for diesel cars [READ]. — [TU Graz 2013](https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/6207922a2acc01004530a67e/625e7ef73f2c1259c56737b4_HBEFA32_EF_Euro_5_6_TUG.pdf)
- Worked speed cases from HBEFA 3.2 for a 40 t truck: a motorway at 86.3 km/h free-flow and 66.3 km/h saturated, an urban 70 km/h road at 59.1 free-flow and 38.6 saturated [READ]. These show that average speed and traffic state are entangled in the database. — [TU Graz 2013, Table 20](https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/6207922a2acc01004530a67e/625e7ef73f2c1259c56737b4_HBEFA32_EF_Euro_5_6_TUG.pdf)

### Inferences
- The answer to the critical question is "not established, and probably not monotonic". The TNO table shows rural normal (202) below motorway 80 km/h (260), and both below urban and congested (237-379), for pre-RDE Euro 6 diesel. It compares road types with very different dynamics, so it does not show what a lower limit would do on a given road.
- Average-speed curves have a well-known trap: low mean speed in the database usually means congestion and stop-start, so emissions at low mean speed are not the emissions of steady 50 km/h driving. Applying COPERT or HBEFA curves to a posted limit in free flow is outside the guidebook's stated conditions.
- For trucks, evidence suggests low engine load is the risk, so steady slower running on an 80 km/h road could raise NOx for SCR-equipped trucks. I found no direct measurement of this in the 40-70 km/h range for a limit change.
- A conservative default for the advisor: treat NOx benefit as decreasing to zero somewhere around 60-70 km/h, do not claim a benefit at 40-50 km/h on air-quality grounds, and treat exhaust PM10 as independent of speed for DPF-equipped fleets. These are engineering judgements, not measured results.

## How much of roadside PM10 is exhaust versus non-exhaust, and how much can a speed limit change it?

### Takeaway
Non-exhaust is now the majority of road-transport primary PM10 (73% in the UK inventory for 2016). Measured roadside splits put exhaust at around 40-55%, mostly from older fleets. Speed limits changed total roadside PM10 by at most about 0.4-2 µg/m³ in the evaluations found, against typical motorway contributions of 2-5 µg/m³.

### Cited Findings
- UK inventory: non-exhaust was 39% of road transport PM10 in 2000 and 73% in 2016, projected at 94% by 2030 without abatement. It was 8.5% of total UK primary PM10 in 2016 [READ]. — [AQEG, Non-Exhaust Emissions from Road Traffic, Defra PB14581, 2019](https://uk-air.defra.gov.uk/assets/documents/reports/cat09/1907101151_20190709_Non_Exhaust_Emissions_typeset_Final.pdf)
- London Marylebone Road: traffic increment of PM10 10.3 over five years, carbonaceous portion about 55%, so non-exhaust about 45%. Berlin: 55% of the roadside PM10 increment was exhaust plus tyre abrasion, 45% resuspension (Lenschow 2001). European cities: exhaust and non-exhaust each about half of the roadside increment (Querol 2004) [READ in AQEG, the underlying papers only quoted]. — [AQEG 2019](https://uk-air.defra.gov.uk/assets/documents/reports/cat09/1907101151_20190709_Non_Exhaust_Emissions_typeset_Final.pdf)
- Swiss roadside, split of traffic-related PM10: freeway 3% brake, 56% resuspended dust, 41% exhaust (about 50 mg/km per light vehicle, 288 mg/km per heavy vehicle); urban canyon 21% brake, 38% resuspension, 41% exhaust (24 and 498 mg/km). No significant tyre-wear or undamaged-pavement contribution was found [ABSTRACT]. — [Bukowiecki et al., 2010, DOI 10.1016/j.atmosenv.2010.03.039](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.atmosenv.2010.03.039)
- Guidebook speed correction for tyre wear (normalised to 80 km/h): ST(V) = 1.39 below 40 km/h; -0.00974·V + 1.78 for 40-90 km/h; 0.902 above 90 km/h. For brake wear (normalised to 65 km/h): SB(V) = 1.67 below 40; -0.0270·V + 2.75 for 40-95 km/h; 0.185 above 95. Both derive from passenger-car data, apply to mean trip speed, and flatten outside the range because of "the absence of any experimental data". Road surface wear is independent of speed, and the chapter gives no resuspension method [READ]. — [EMEP/EEA Guidebook 2023, Update 2023, chapter 1.A.3.b.vi-vii, Eqs. 5 and 8](https://copert.emisia.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/1.A.3.b.vi-vii-Road-tyre-and-brake-wear-2023_Sep.pdf)

|---|---|---|

| 50 | 1.29 | 2.37 |
| 60 | 1.20 | 1.92 |
| 70 | 1.10 | 1.46 |

- AQEG gives brake wear about 9 times higher at urban driving than at free-flowing motorway conditions, against 1.5 times for tyres [READ]. AQEG also lists lowering speed where traffic is free-flowing (trunk roads, motorways) as among the most effective non-exhaust mitigations, and separately suggests reducing braking and high-speed cornering [READ]. — [AQEG 2019](https://uk-air.defra.gov.uk/assets/documents/reports/cat09/1907101151_20190709_Non_Exhaust_Emissions_typeset_Final.pdf)
- Speed-limit effects on PM10, by study: A13 -1.4 at 50 m and -0.4 at 200 m (calculated annual mean), Amsterdam ring -2.20 (control section -0.97), A12 Imst -6% total PM10 emissions from all traffic, Gonçalves up to -3.0% (model), Bel and Rosell +5.3 to +5.9%, Ticino -1 to -3% (one week). See the country sections for sources and tags.
- On speed dependence of non-exhaust, measurements disagree: one study finds factors depend on speed and road wetness, and another finds no significant speed dependence on motorways and major arterials [QUOTED, from a search summary; I could not confirm which statement belongs to which document, and the link is the likeliest source of the second]. — [Harmo conference slides, Lohmeyer](https://www.harmo.org/Conferences/Proceedings/_Garmisch-Partenkirchen/publishedSections/PPT/2.06-Lohmeyer.pdf)

### Inferences
- The guidebook's rising wear factors at low mean speed reflect more braking and cornering in urban driving, not a physical effect of steady slower travel. Applying that table to a limit reduction would wrongly predict more PM10 at 50 km/h on a free-flowing road. Conversely, AQEG's "lower the speed" advice concerns high free-flow speeds, not an 80 km/h road going to 50.
- Plausible change in road-attributable PM10 from any of 40-70 km/h on an 80 km/h road is small and of uncertain sign, and in total concentration probably well under 1 µg/m³. I would present the PM10 effect as "not reliably distinguishable from zero".
- Older roadside splits overstate today's exhaust share: DPFs have since removed most exhaust PM mass, which makes exhaust-based speed benefits (such as Imst -18%) obsolete for modern fleets.
- A PM10 threshold exceedance should therefore not be presented as something the speed advisory can fix. It may be shown as context, while NO2 and NOx drive the logic.

### Gaps
- The guidebook's non-exhaust chapter was revised in September 2025 (brake wear factors, per the update log, seen in a search result); I read the 2023 version and the speed corrections may have changed.
- I found no measured split for a modern fleet on an 80 km/h non-urban road, and no direct measurement of resuspension versus speed at 40-80 km/h in my reading.

## What transfers to an 80 km/h road with candidate limits of 40 to 80 km/h?

### Takeaway
Almost nothing is directly transferable below 80 km/h. Nearly all evaluations cover cuts from 100-130 km/h to 80-100 km/h on motorways. The only sub-80 evidence I found is Madrid's 90 to 70 km/h change, confounded by a road rebuild, and urban 20 mph work with an old fleet.

### Cited Findings
- Evidence coverage by speed step, all figures from the sections above:

| Step | Best available evidence | NOx or NO2 | PM10 | Quality |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 130/110 to 100 | Tyrol A12; Netherlands 2020 | NOx emissions -11 to -13% ([DERIVED], Tyrol); -6 to -9% (NL cars and vans) | None | Model-assisted |
| 100 to 80 | Salzburg A1; Dutch zones; Barcelona | -5.6% emissions, NO2 -3 to -4 (Salzburg); NOx -5 to -30% (Keuken); +1.7 to +3.2% (Bel and Rosell) | -5 to -25% emissions (Keuken); -1.4 to -2.2 concentration; +5.3 to +5.9% (Bel and Rosell) | Mixed, some observational |
| 90 to 70 | Madrid M-30 | NOx -16.4% | None | Confounded by redesign, abstract only |
| 80 to 70 | None found | Unknown | Unknown | No evidence |
| 80 to 60, 50, 40 | None found for rural or free-flow roads; London 20 mph is the only related work | Petrol +7.9%, diesel -8.2% (Euro IV, [QUOTED]) | -8.3% ([QUOTED]) | Weak, different context |

### Inferences
- Do not extrapolate 100-to-80 percentages to 80-to-60. The available data suggest the curve may flatten or reverse in that range, but I could not obtain a verified curve, so a constant gain per km/h has no support.
- The fleet composition at the deployment site matters more than the policy case. Most reductions in the older literature are diesel-car effects from 2003-2017 fleets, while Euro 6d cars and Euro VI trucks are much cleaner.
- Effect sizes are near-road. Dutch evidence shows the effect roughly halving or worse between 50 m and 200 m, and it is negligible at 1,000 m or more, so a recommendation should depend on receptors close to the road.
- Suggested handling in the advisor, as a design judgement: (1) trigger on NO2 or NOx, not PM10; (2) allow an air-quality reduction to 70 km/h at most, unless local measurements support going lower; (3) model effects on realised speed, not the posted limit, scaled by about 0.7 of theoretical (Salzburg); (4) display uncertainty, because evidence below 80 km/h is sparse; (5) justify limits of 40-60 km/h on safety or noise grounds rather than air quality.

### Gaps
- Evidence for ordinary 80 km/h rural or arterial roads is absent from what I could find. Local before/after monitoring would be needed to support any air-quality claim below 70 km/h.
- Many peer-reviewed full texts (Keller 2008, Gonçalves 2008, Baldasano 2010, Keuken 2010, Dijkema 2008, Perez-Prada 2017) were available to me only as abstracts or records.
