# Ice justifies lower limits; air-quality evidence stops at 80 km/h

No published study tests a 40, 50, 60 or 70 km/h limit on an 80 km/h road under darkness, black ice and poor air quality, so every number a "Research suggests this" panel shows is a transfer from another setting. The strongest case for going below 80 km/h is black ice, and it rests mainly on stopping-distance physics: matching the dry-road stopping distance from 80 km/h at a friction of 0.15 needs about 46 km/h (calculation), while drivers on slippery roads shed only about 4 to 9 km/h unprompted in most of the studies read. Crash models then add a modest, uncertain benefit, because they respond to realised mean speed, and a posted cut reaches mean speed at about one quarter strength (R² 0.49). On that basis 80 to 60 km/h means roughly 5 km/h less mean speed and, by calculation, about 16 to 26% fewer injury crashes and 28 to 33% fewer deaths, with the spread driven mostly by which published coefficient is used. Darkness alone has the weakest case: risk is 1.3 to 1.5 times higher after controlling for time of day, yet two Finnish systems tried a darkness rule and abandoned it, and no controlled study shows a darkness-only limit reducing crashes in general traffic. Air-quality evidence covers cuts to 80 km/h, not below it, and PM10 effects are too small to distinguish from zero, so a PM10 exceedance should be context and not a justification. No published model relates speed to near-accidents, which means crash coefficients must not be applied to them. Controlled crash evidence for weather-responsive limits exists but is mostly non-significant or uncontrolled, and the panel should present condition classes, a stopping-distance check and uncertain ranges rather than a predicted crash rate.

## Choice of coefficient matters more than choice of model

Both models apply to **mean traffic speed**, not to the posted limit. Nilsson's Power Model is crashes_after / crashes_before = (v_after / v_before)^n, with the classic exponents 2 for all injury accidents, 3 for fatal and serious accidents and 4 for fatal accidents ([Nilsson 2004](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/4394446/1693353.pdf) [P]). The 3 was a reasoned choice between 2 and 4, not an estimate, the thesis gives no property-damage exponent, and it says the model underestimates fatalities ([Nilsson 2004](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/4394446/1693353.pdf) [P]). The Exponential Model is crashes_after / crashes_before = exp(β · (v_after − v_before)) ([Elvik 2014](https://www.toi.no/getfile.php/1335297-1394518277/Publikasjoner/T%C3%98I%20rapporter/2014/1296-2014/1296-2014-elektronisk.pdf) [P]; [Ambros and Kieć 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12544-025-00753-6) [P]).

The practical dispute is the injury-crash coefficient. **The β of 0.034 and the quoted 2019 β of 0.06 differ by a factor of about 1.8, and that gap is larger than the difference between the two model forms at these speeds.** The 2019 table could not be read, so 0.06 cannot be confirmed as an injury-crash value. Elvik's own 2018 slides attach 0.06 to serious injury and 0.04 to slight injury ([Elvik 2018](https://api.sambuz.com/doc/speed-is-an-important-risk-factor-a-comprehensive-and-pdf-document-539697) [P\*], a third-party copy), which is a different quantity from injury crashes. Exponents also trended down through 2009 ([Elvik 2009](https://www.toi.no/getfile.php/1313212-1259661631/Publikasjoner/T%C3%98I%20rapporter/2009/1034-2009/1034-2009-Sum.pdf) [P]), and the 2019 values then go the other way for post-2000 studies. The Finnish appraisal rule of 2.5% fewer injury accidents per 1 km/h reduction ([Rämä et al. 2003](https://www.tieh.fi/julkaisut/pdf/3200841_vms-turva.pdf) [Q]) corresponds to β of about 0.025 [C], below both.

**Neither model wins in the 40 to 80 km/h band.** The 2019 update says both "fit the data very well" ([Elvik et al. 2019](https://trid.trb.org/View/1569665) [A]). Elvik 2014 finds the Exponential clearly better for injury and PDO accidents and roughly equal for fatal accidents, with Power "possibly a little better" ([Elvik 2014](https://www.toi.no/getfile.php/1335297-1394518277/Publikasjoner/T%C3%98I%20rapporter/2014/1296-2014/1296-2014-elektronisk.pdf) [P]). The Power Model's constant exponent implies the same percentage effect from 100 to 80 km/h as from 10 to 8 km/h, which Elvik calls unreasonable, and changes below about 60 km/h tend to have smaller effects than changes above it ([Elvik 2009](https://www.toi.no/getfile.php/1313212-1259661631/Publikasjoner/T%C3%98I%20rapporter/2009/1034-2009/1034-2009-Sum.pdf) [P]). In the one fitted fatal-accident table available, the two models err in opposite directions where it matters: at 55 to 75 km/h the Power fit sits above the data and the Exponential below it, and the Exponential overpredicts the 115 km/h bin by about 82% ([Elvik 2014 Table 4.2](https://www.toi.no/getfile.php/1335297-1394518277/Publikasjoner/T%C3%98I%20rapporter/2014/1296-2014/1296-2014-elektronisk.pdf) [P]). The Exponential is the better engineering default for this road because it gives a constant percentage per km/h, stays well behaved at low speed and can model a change in part of the speed distribution. That preference is a judgement, and the two forms are linked by a local equivalence n = β·v [C], so β 0.065 acts like an exponent of 2.6 at 40 km/h and 5.2 at 80 km/h.

Road type matters and is thinly covered. Elvik 2009 pools rural roads with freeways, and its rural serious-injury-accident cell (2.6, CI −2.7 to 7.9) is close to uninformative ([Elvik 2009](https://www.toi.no/getfile.php/1313212-1259661631/Publikasjoner/T%C3%98I%20rapporter/2009/1034-2009/1034-2009-Sum.pdf) [P]). Cameron and Elvik found powers for serious casualties lower on urban arterials than rural highways and lower on rural highways than freeways, but their numeric values were not retrieved ([Cameron and Elvik 2010](https://www.science.gov/topicpages/n/nilsson+model.html) [A]). No exponential coefficient by road type was found. At site level the models perform poorly: in 238 low-speed locations, expected and observed crash changes showed "almost no correlation", and the authors warn against using the output as a predictor of exact values ([Ambros and Kieć 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12544-025-00753-6) [P]).

## A posted cut reaches mean speed at about one quarter strength

The central conversion is from Elvik et al.: **Δmean = 0.2525 · Δlimit − 1.2204, R² = 0.487**, summarised by the authors as "around 25%" ([Elvik et al. 2004](https://www.trg.dk/elvik/740-2004.pdf) [P]). The source passage does not explain the intercept of −1.2 km/h, so the slope alone is the defensible part. Observed ratios range widely, and the highest come from motorways with mean speeds far above the new limit or from winter periods where weather is entangled with the sign.

Wyoming's high ratios deserve caution. The Sui and Young regression puts drivers 1.3 mph below the maximum when it is posted, and 5.4, 8.5 and about 13 mph above the limit for reductions of up to 15, 16 to 25 and 30 or more mph ([Sui and Young 2013](https://www.westernite.org/annualmeetings/13_Phoenix/Papers/Session%205D%20-%20Sui.pdf) [P]). Those levels imply a fall of about 0.5 mph per mph at all three sizes [C], but drivers were also slowing for the weather itself. The Finnish studies isolate the limit from the weather effect and give 0.17 to 0.19. **Compliance also worsens as the cut gets deeper**, which matters directly for a jump from 80 to 40 km/h. A deep cut leaves 13 mph of excess at Elk Mountain, and the Phase II report records a "reduction in speed compliance related to large reductions in posted speed limits" ([Young et al. 2012](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/29333) [A]).

Advisory status is a real gap. FHWA says VSL success "relies to a significant degree on driver compliance" and that compliance is "typically low" when limits are not enforceable ([FHWA 2017](https://ops.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/fhwahop17003/es.htm) [P\*]; [FHWA deck 2024](https://tmcpfs.ops.fhwa.dot.gov/pdfs/TMS_Managing_Use_VSLs_Adverse_Weather.pdf) [P]). **No published ratio exists for an advisory display on an 80 km/h road.** The Dutch 80 km/h zones and the Salzburg results also bundle a limit with enforcement or section control. The ratio of 0.25 should therefore be treated as an upper-leaning default for a sign without enforcement and exposed as a calibration parameter.

An alternative route skips mean speed altogether. Musicant et al. fitted crash ratio = (limit_after / limit_before)^k, with k = 1.24 (SD 0.44) for reductions ([Musicant et al. 2016](https://trid.trb.org/View/1393349) [A]; crash severity is not stated in the abstract). A real-world cross-check is encouraging but loose. A 3.8 km/h mean-speed fall implies about 12% fewer injury accidents at β 0.034, 20% at β 0.06 and 26% fewer deaths at β 0.08 [C]. The 3.8 km/h comes from 1987 to 1989, and that re-analysis covers 2010 to 2014, so the agreement is suggestive only.

## Black ice needs about 46 km/h, and drivers shed far less on their own

Physics supplies the most defensible logic in this report because it does not depend on driver compliance. The form is D = v·t/3.6 + v²/(254·μ), with grade added to μ [C, standard physics]. AASHTO's design check uses t = 2.5 s and a deceleration of 3.4 m/s², reproduced in a state manual ([Iowa DOT 6D-1](https://documents.iowa.gov/home/download/9592894) [P of manual, Q for AASHTO]). That 3.4 m/s² equals μ of about 0.35 [C] and is a chosen comfort value since the 2001 edition, not a friction measurement ([Cheng et al. 2011](https://bjrbe-journals.rtu.lv/bjrbe/article/download/bjrbe.2011.13/127/364) [P of review, Q for AASHTO and NCHRP 400]). On packed snow and ice the available grip is below 3.4 m/s², so AASHTO distances are unsafe unless μ replaces the deceleration term.

**Black ice has no single friction value.** The Swedish review reports skiddometer values at 17% slip: dry 0.8 to 1.0, wet 0.7 to 0.8, packed snow 0.20 to 0.30, black ice 0.15 to 0.30 and wet black ice 0.05 to 0.10 ([Wallman and Åström 2001](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:673366/FULLTEXT01.pdf) [P]). Instrumented tyre tests on prepared ice at 4 kN gave 0.078 for winter tyres, 0.077 for summer tyres and 0.16 for studded tyres, with the report warning that the ice results have a large spread ([Svendenius 2007](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/4590032/8627412.pdf) [P]). The same review says the span within one road condition is often larger than the difference between condition means, and that international values are "not directly comparable" ([Wallman and Åström 2001](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:673366/FULLTEXT01.pdf) [P]). Skiddometer numbers are device values and overstate what a real car achieves by an unknown amount. Using 0.15 as a default with a 0.05 to 0.08 stress case is defensible as a modelling choice.

Reaction time is less settled than its design use suggests. A value of 2.5 s covers about 90% of drivers in the studies a review cites ([Cheng et al. 2011](https://bjrbe-journals.rtu.lv/bjrbe/article/download/bjrbe.2011.13/127/364) [P of review, Q for studies]). Lower figures of 0.7 to 0.75 s expected, 1.25 s unexpected and 1.5 s for surprise events come from [Green 2000](https://drivingresearch.pages.dev/catalog/green-2000-how-long-does-it-take-to-stop/) [S], and a 95th percentile of about 1.6 s comes from [Olson and Sivak 1986](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3710489/) [S], which commentators call a best case ([Green, "Let's Get Real"](https://www.visualexpert.com/Resources/realprt.html) [S]). **No verified night-specific reaction time was found**, so darkness should enter through detection distance and not through an invented longer t.

Darkness caps the distance available. AAA measured low beams revealing a non-reflective object at 300 ft (91 m) for halogen reflectors, 400 ft for halogen projector and HID, and 450 ft (137 m) for LED, with object size and contrast unstated ([AAA 2015](https://newsroom.aaa.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/AET-Headlights-Fact-Sheet.pdf) [P]). VTI's own verdict is that winter risk-friction estimates are "few and unreliable" ([Wallman and Åström 2001](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:673366/FULLTEXT01.pdf) [P]). These ratios should rank severity and not feed a formula. **Ice and darkness combine at less than the product of the two.** In the Swedish table, ice or snow plus darkness against bare daylight is 5.1, 8.5 and 16.2 in the north, centre and south, against a multiplicative prediction of 8.2, 13.3 and 20.0 [C from Table 4.1, P], so an advisory score must not multiply them.

Drivers do not close the gap unprompted. Weather and surface alone lowered free-flow car speed by 6.3 km/h on average on the Finnish E18, and by 9.3 km/h when slippery-road signs appeared ([Rämä 1999](https://cris.vtt.fi/en/publications/effects-of-weather-controlled-variable-speed-limits-and-warning-s/) [A]). Finnish drivers on slippery roads averaged only about 4 km/h slower than in good winter conditions, and only 14% judged the road slippery ([Rämä 2001](https://sarjaweb.vtt.fi/pdf/publications/2001/P447.pdf) [Q]). Limit changes were also weaker on ice: when Sweden went from 110 to 90 km/h, predicted speeds fell about 7 km/h on dry roads and about 4 km/h on icy roads ([Nilsson 1984](https://trid.trb.org/View/204558) [A]). A 1977 Swedish study reported larger falls, 13 km/h in the slow lane and 18 km/h for fast drivers, and said that keeping the dry stopping distance would have required up to 30 to 35 km/h less speed ([TRID 92005](https://trid.trb.org/View/92005) [S]), close to the 34 km/h drop calculated above. Most of this evidence predates widespread ABS and ESC.

## Deployed systems stop near 60 km/h, and controlled crash proof is thin

On roads with a 100 km/h base, deployed regulatory limits under ice or snow cluster at **80 km/h for moderate slipperiness and 60 km/h for very poor conditions**. Finnish systems use 80 for snow or ice with the surface below +2 °C, and 60 for visibility under 100 m, freezing rain or exceptional conditions ([Rämä et al. 2003, Appendices 1 to 3](https://www.tieh.fi/julkaisut/pdf/3200841_vms-turva.pdf) [P\*, column reading from extracted text]). The Netherlands' A16 showed 80 km/h at 70 to 140 m visibility and 60 km/h below 70 m ([Hogema et al. 1994](https://open.rijkswaterstaat.nl/publish/pages/153378/70142.pdf) [P]). US freeways step down from 65 to 75 mph to 30 to 35 mph (48 to 56 km/h). Washington's table is 55 mph for ice in places, 45 mph for snow and ice and 35 mph for severe freezing rain ([FHWA deck 2024](https://tmcpfs.ops.fhwa.dot.gov/pdfs/TMS_Managing_Use_VSLs_Adverse_Weather.pdf) [P, citing an unpublished 2024 WSDOT document]). Colorado's physics-derived values for ice, 25 to 35 mph, sit far below European practice ([CDOT 2023](https://www.codot.gov/safety/traffic-safety/assets/variable-speed-limits/cdot-variable-speed-limits-guidelines_2023-11-02.pdf) [P]). Operators appear to post what drivers will accept, not what preserves stopping distance, which is an interpretation the sources do not state. **Black ice as a named condition appears in only one document read**, Central Finland's autumn rule at 80 km/h on a 100 km/h road.

Direct precedents on an 80 km/h base exist but lack effect data. Finland lowers about 180 km of 80 km/h road to 70 and about 3 km to 60 each winter in Northern Ostrobothnia and Kainuu ([press release 2026](https://www.sttinfo.fi/tiedote/72447134/talvi-ja-pimean-ajan-nopeusrajoitukset-tulevat-voimaan-talla-viikolla?lang=fi) [P]), and two spot sites drop weather-triggered from 80 to 60 and 80 to 50 ([Rämä et al. 2003](https://www.tieh.fi/julkaisut/pdf/3200841_vms-turva.pdf) [P]). No effect evaluation of those sites was found.

Regression to the mean is a live risk: most systems were sited after bad crash histories or a triggering pile-up, and none of the before-after studies read adjusts for it. The A16 result sets a design constraint. Below 35 m visibility the 60 km/h display exceeded what drivers would choose unprompted, and speeds rose. A floor display can therefore raise speeds when conditions are worse than the display assumes. That argues for warnings or lower values at the extreme. Sweden's reported fall of 12 to 20 km/h on ice at weather-controlled sites ([Gustafsson et al. 2012](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:669257/FULLTEXT01.pdf) [Q]) is far larger than Finland's 1.8 km/h with slippery signs, but it reaches us second-hand and should not be used as a design value.

**No deployment states or validates a maximum acceptable crash rate.** Colorado uses safety performance functions to select corridors, then derives the limit from friction and grade ([CDOT 2023](https://www.codot.gov/safety/traffic-safety/assets/variable-speed-limits/cdot-variable-speed-limits-guidelines_2023-11-02.pdf) [P]), and FHWA notes that "supporting data may not provide for clear recommendations" ([FHWA deck 2024](https://tmcpfs.ops.fhwa.dot.gov/pdfs/TMS_Managing_Use_VSLs_Adverse_Weather.pdf) [P]). Germany automates visibility and precipitation, but road slipperiness is only a switching proposal and slipperiness warnings are manual ([Haug and Busch 2016](https://api.lambrecht.net/upload/productDocuments/Umfelddatentestfeld_Abschlussbericht_3.pdf) [P]), so a fully automatic ice response goes beyond German practice. One Finnish control rule is worth copying: the limit worsens immediately and improves only after three consecutive better recommendations, at least 15 minutes ([Rämä et al. 2003](https://www.tieh.fi/julkaisut/pdf/3200841_vms-turva.pdf) [P]).

### Darkness alone has the weakest case

Two Finnish systems, road 1 at Salo and road 9 at Tampere-Orivesi, lowered limits to 80 km/h for darkness and then abandoned the rule, with no reason or effect reported ([Rämä et al. 2003](https://www.tieh.fi/julkaisut/pdf/3200841_vms-turva.pdf) [P]). The same report still says that lowering two-lane roads to 80 km/h in winter darkness "could be justified", a recommendation and not a tested measure. In Wyoming a 70 to 55 mph dusk-to-dawn limit, with flashing beacons, moved speeds only 3 to 5 mph, and the authors judged it not effective unless other measures change speeds ([Riginos et al. 2019](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/42488/dot_42488_DS1.pdf) [P], a wildlife study, so general crash effects were not measured). Central Finland's combined rule, "heavy rain in darkness" giving 80 km/h, is the better model: **darkness is more defensible as a modifier of wet or slippery conditions than as a stand-alone trigger.**

**The Barcelona conflict is the most important dispute here.** Models show small gains, while station data show none or a slight worsening, and the same observational studies credit a **variable-speed system with NOx −7.7 to −17.1% and PM10 −14.5 to −17.3%** ([Bel and Rosell 2013](https://hdl.handle.net/2445/44528) [P]; consistent with [Bel et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.trd.2015.02.003) [A]). Models cannot show congestion, compliance or displacement effects, which is the likely reason for the split. The +5% PM10 reads best as "no detectable benefit", not as proof that lower speeds raise emissions. The variable-speed roads were also selected, so this is not clean causal proof that dynamic operation is better.

The speed-emission curve below 80 km/h is unverified. The COPERT coefficients and HBEFA tables could not be retrieved. The guidebook form exists but extrapolation is "not advisable", and the factors apply only to average travelling speed, not spot or constant speeds ([EMEP/EEA Guidebook 2025](https://copert.emisia.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/1.A.3.b.i-iv-Road-transport-2025.pdf) [P]). One road-type table for pre-RDE Euro 6 diesel cars gives NOx of 202 mg/km for rural normal driving and 260 at motorway 80 km/h, with urban and congested values of 237 to 379 ([TU Graz 2013](https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/6207922a2acc01004530a67e/625e7ef73f2c1259c56737b4_HBEFA32_EF_Euro_5_6_TUG.pdf) [P]). That is consistent with a U-shape but compares different driving patterns on different road types. For heavy vehicles, SCR is less active at low exhaust temperature ([TU Graz 2013](https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/6207922a2acc01004530a67e/625e7ef73f2c1259c56737b4_HBEFA32_EF_Euro_5_6_TUG.pdf) [P]), which gives a mechanism for higher NOx at low load, though no measurement of this for a limit change at 40 to 70 km/h was found.

**PM10 is the wrong trigger.** Non-exhaust emissions were 73% of UK road-transport PM10 in 2016 ([AQEG 2019](https://uk-air.defra.gov.uk/assets/documents/reports/cat09/1907101151_20190709_Non_Exhaust_Emissions_typeset_Final.pdf) [P]), Euro VI exhaust PM factors are "close to zero" ([TU Graz 2013](https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/6207922a2acc01004530a67e/625e7ef73f2c1259c56737b4_HBEFA32_EF_Euro_5_6_TUG.pdf) [P]), and motorway contribution to total PM10 at Dutch hotspots was only 6 to 14% [C from [Rijkswaterstaat 2011](https://open.rijkswaterstaat.nl/publish/pages/7841/16-verhoging_maximumsnelheid_80-km_zones_naar_100_kmh_effecten_op_luchtkwaliteit_rijkswaterstaat.pdf), P]. During the Ticino PM10 episode, an estimated 60 to 70% of winter PM10 was secondary ([Ticino 2008](https://m4.ti.ch/fileadmin/DT/temi/oasi/documenti/Rapporto_introduzione_80kmh_2008.pdf) [P]). Ticino's trigger of PM10 above 100 µg/m³ at five stations, lifted below 50, is the only numeric trigger verified, and Tyrol and Salzburg describe theirs only qualitatively ([Thudium and Chélala 2015](https://www.tirol.gv.at/fileadmin/buergerservice/kundmachungen/umweltschutz/ALuft/160120_thu_Be_T100PermanentTirol2015finalV2.pdf) [P]). The guidebook's tyre and brake speed corrections rise to 1.39 and 2.83 at 40 km/h relative to 80 [C from [Guidebook 2023](https://copert.emisia.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/1.A.3.b.vi-vii-Road-tyre-and-brake-wear-2023_Sep.pdf), P], but they reflect urban braking at low mean trip speed. Applying them to a limit change would wrongly predict more PM10 on a free-flowing road.

Effects are also near-road and shrinking. Dutch figures roughly halve or worse between 50 and 200 m, and the ministry calls speed effects negligible at 1,000 m or more ([letter 2012](https://zoek.officielebekendmakingen.nl/blg-153141.pdf) [P]). The Salzburg NO2 effect fell from 1.8 to about 1 µg/m³ within a few years as baseline NO2 and the speed gap shrank. Euro 6d-temp diesel cars averaged about 44 mg/km hot NOx ([Weller et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmech.2019.00045) [P\*]), roughly 5 to 10 times below the pre-RDE values above [C]. The researcher's design judgement, which is not a finding, is to trigger on NO2 or NOx, cap any air-quality reduction at 70 km/h unless local monitoring supports going lower, and justify 40 to 60 km/h on safety grounds alone.

## No published model links speed to near-accidents

**No study was found estimating a Power or Exponential relationship between speed and near-accidents, conflicts or surrogate measures, and no field-observed conflict study of a limit change was found.** The closest evidence is microsimulation. On a VISSIM and SSAM motorway model, total conflicts fell from 11,544 to 10,742 with one variable-message sign and to 8,785 with a sign every 2 km, while network mean speed barely moved, 64.52 to 63.60 km/h ([Oskarbski et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7571130/) [P\*]). Conflicts changing without a matching mean-speed change is the opposite of what a mean-speed model predicts, which is a further reason not to transfer the coefficients.

Near-crash validity is itself contested. Near-crashes occur about 10 to 15 times as often as crashes, and the assumption of identical causal mechanisms is hard to evaluate ([NHTSA DOT HS 811 382](https://www.nhtsa.gov/sites/nhtsa.gov/files/documents/dot_hs_811_382.pdf) [S]). No single surrogate measure performs well on all dimensions ([Lu et al. 2021](https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:a1963b16-ade9-44ff-8be3-fb97f9ef9199) [S]). The SHRP2 odds ratio of 12.8 for speeding, and Young's split into 5.4 for exceeding the limit and 71.5 for exceeding a safe speed without exceeding the limit, are search-excerpt only and describe crashes, not near-crashes ([Young 2017](https://mdpi.com/2313-576X/3/4/28) [S]). Near-accidents need a separate, locally measured model. Speed variance offers no shortcut: Elvik regards the harm of high variation as "beyond reasonable doubt" but says it cannot yet be expressed as a function, and the crashes affected are probably mostly PDO ([Elvik 2014](https://www.toi.no/getfile.php/1335297-1394518277/Publikasjoner/T%C3%98I%20rapporter/2014/1296-2014/1296-2014-elektronisk.pdf) [P]). A variance multiplier would need an invented coefficient.

## Conclusion

The literature supports a narrower claim than a speed-limit recommender would like to make. It can say how far a vehicle travels before stopping on a given surface, and it can give a rough, uncertain crash reduction for a realised speed change, but it cannot say what crash or near-accident rate a displayed number will produce on this road. The most useful consequence is architectural: treat physics as the deciding layer for ice, treat crash coefficients as an explanatory range, and keep darkness as a modifier, air quality as a NOx and NO2 concern with a low ceiling on claims, and near-accidents as a locally measured quantity.

The most valuable next reads are few and specific. Reading the Elvik 2019 table would remove the largest numeric ambiguity, confirming the Norwegian 80 to 60 km/h case would give both the closest compliance match and a possible air-quality data point below 80 km/h, and a short field measurement of mean speed by displayed value would replace the one parameter the entire crash calculation depends on and no study has measured for an advisory panel.
